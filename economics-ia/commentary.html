Research question: To what extent will the scarcity of energy and fertiliser caused by disruptions to the Strait of Hormuz contribute to global food-price inflation?

Definition

Scarcity — finite resources cannot satisfy unlimited wants, so every choice carries an opportunity cost — is the key concept here. Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz has not destroyed the world's energy and fertiliser but has blocked access to them, so relative scarcity has risen. Both are intermediate goods in derived demand: farmers buy urea for the cereals it yields, not for itself. When such factors of production become scarcer, firms' costs of production rise, short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) contracts and the economy suffers cost-push inflation: a sustained rise in the average price level caused by rising costs, not excess aggregate demand.

Evidence

The strait "normally carries about one-third of global seaborne fertiliser and one-quarter of seaborne oil", so its closure is a negative supply-side shock at a chokepoint. Brent crude rose from $61 to $118 a barrel in the first quarter of 2026, and urea passed $850 a tonne in April, an 80% rise in two months. The World Bank now projects the fertiliser price index to rise by more than 30% in 2026. The FAO expects fertiliser prices to average 20% higher in the first half of 2026, and the World Food Programme estimates 45 million more people could face acute food shortages if oil stays above $100 a barrel.

Example and diagram: the market for fertiliser

Natural gas is the principal variable cost of ammonia and urea, so energy scarcity and blocked shipping reinforce one another: Iran halted ammonia output, Qatar suspended urea and sulphur production, India cut output as LNG supplies fell. In Figure 1, supply decreases from S₁ to S₂. Because nitrogen has no substitute within a single planting season, demand is price-inelastic (PED < 1), so price rises sharply from P₁ to P₂ while quantity falls only from Q₁ to Q₂: almost the whole adjustment is borne by price.

Diagram: transmission to the food market

Fertiliser, fuel and freight are farming inputs, so dearer inputs raise costs of production and supply in the global wheat market contracts from S₁ to S₂ (Figure 2). Food is a necessity with few substitutes, so demand is again inelastic and price rises proportionately more than quantity falls. Here the price mechanism performs its rationing function: scarce fertiliser is allocated to the highest-value users, while poorer farmers are priced out. Farmers also apply less fertiliser, lowering yields: the FAO expects wheat, maize and rice output below 2025's records, shifting supply leftward again next harvest.

Diagram: the macroeconomic outcome

Food and energy are heavily weighted in consumer price indices, so these microeconomic rises aggregate. In Figure 3 the shock shifts SRAS₁ to SRAS₂: the average price level rises from PL₁ to PL₂ while real output falls from Yf to Y₂, opening a negative output gap. Inflation alongside falling real output is stagflation, and the IMF has raised its forecast for global headline inflation from 4.1% in 2025 to 4.7% in 2026, "driven mainly by higher energy and food prices". For net food-importing economies the shock also worsens the terms of trade, cutting real national income.

Evaluation

Three arguments suggest a large contribution. First, low PED and low short-run price elasticity of supply mean price absorbs the shock. Second, energy enters every stage of the food chain — cultivation, processing, transport, refrigeration — so the shock is economy-wide, not confined to one market. Third, the incidence is regressive: by Engel's law, poor households spend a high proportion of income on food, so their real incomes fall furthest, and because staple demand is income-inelastic, falling incomes do little to curb it. Matin Qaim (University of Bonn) predicts "poor people in Africa and Asia will be hurt the most", with India, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Somalia, Sudan, Tanzania, Kenya and Egypt most at risk.

Yet the extent depends on duration. Analysts agree the "true impact of the conflict has yet to be felt", given the time lag between input costs and retail prices: the FAO Food Price Index averaged only 130.7 points in April 2026, 2% above a year earlier, as good harvests and stocks cushioned consumers. Fertiliser and energy are only one component of food costs, and supply becomes more elastic over time as producers substitute manure, re-route to Russian or North African suppliers and add capacity, shifting supply rightward. Attribution is also difficult: weather, El Niño and export restrictions are confounding causes, making the closure a necessary but insufficient cause. Notably, core inflation stayed stable while headline inflation jumped, implying a change in relative prices rather than generalised inflation.

In conclusion, the contribution is significant but conditional. If the strait reopens, the outcome is a one-off rise in the price level, not sustained inflation, provided inflationary expectations stay anchored and no wage-price spiral develops. If disruption continues through the 2026–27 planting season, the lagged yield effect will deliver a second, larger round of food-price inflation, borne most heavily by low-income food-deficit countries — a case for buffer stocks and targeted subsidies rather than price controls.
