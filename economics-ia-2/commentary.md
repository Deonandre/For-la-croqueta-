> **Research question:** To what extent would a sugar tax on sugar-sweetened beverages improve market outcomes in Germany?

---

## Definition

**Intervention** is government action that changes a market outcome in order to correct **market failure** — a misallocation of resources in which a free market does not achieve **allocative efficiency**. Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) create a **negative externality of consumption**: drinking them imposes **external costs** on third parties, because obesity and type-2 diabetes are treated through Germany's statutory health insurance and funded by all contributors. SSBs are therefore **demerit goods**, over-consumed partly because of **information failure**. Since **marginal private benefit (MPB)** exceeds **marginal social benefit (MSB)**, resources are over-allocated and a **welfare loss** arises. A **specific (indirect) tax** is the standard **Pigouvian** correction.

## Evidence

On 27 March 2026 the Bundesrat voted on Schleswig-Holstein's proposal to tax sugary drinks and ban energy-drink sales to under-16s, with revenue **earmarked** for health initiatives; the proposal deliberately left the levy's design open. The WHO reports that more than 100 countries already tax sugary drinks, including about half of EU member states, and a Forsa survey found roughly 60% of Germans in favour. Guenther's own CDU rejected the idea in February, yet backing has since come from parts of the party as well as the Greens and Social Democrats. A 2023 Technical University of Munich study modelled a UK-style levy as cutting daily sugar intake by 2–3 grams, preventing up to 244,000 cases of type-2 diabetes and saving as much as €16 billion over twenty years.

## Example and diagram: the market failure

Greens lawmaker Johannes Wagner framed the case precisely: "Anyone making profits from heavily sugared drinks must also contribute more to financing the resulting costs." Because consumers weigh only private benefit, the market settles where MPB = **marginal private cost (MPC)**, at Qm in Figure 1, rather than at the social optimum Q*, where **marginal social cost (MSC)** = MSB. The vertical gap between MPB and MSB is the **marginal external cost (MEC)**, and the shaded triangle is the **welfare loss** from over-consumption. The €16 billion of avoidable treatment costs is that triangle expressed in euros.

![Figure 1: Negative externality of consumption in the SSB market — MPB lies above MSB by the marginal external cost, so the free market consumes Qm rather than the socially optimum Q*, and the shaded triangle is the resulting welfare loss.](diagrams/fig1-externality.svg)

## Diagram: the tax as intervention

A specific tax raises production costs at every output, shifting supply vertically from S₁ to S₂ (Figure 2). The consumer price rises from P₁ to P₂ while producers retain only P₃, and quantity contracts from Qm towards Q*, **internalising the externality**. The price now reflects the full social cost, restoring **allocative efficiency** where MSB = MSC. The shaded area is government revenue, which the proposal would **hypothecate** to health programmes. **Tax incidence** is split according to relative elasticities: because SSBs have close substitutes — water, juice and sugar-free variants — demand is relatively **price-elastic**, so quantity falls appreciably and producers absorb a meaningful share.

![Figure 2: A specific tax on sugar-sweetened beverages — supply shifts from S1 to S2 by the amount of the tax, raising the consumer price to P2, cutting the producer price to P3 and reducing quantity from Qm towards the social optimum Q*; the shaded rectangle is tax revenue, split into consumer and producer burdens.](diagrams/fig2-specific-tax.svg)

## Diagram: the reformulation channel

Sabine Dittmar argued the levy should be **tiered**, taxing high-sugar drinks more heavily. A tiered levy is avoidable, which is precisely its purpose: Guenther's aim is that "manufacturers should have an incentive to revise their recipes and reduce sugar content. So far they have had no such incentive." In Figure 3 producers reformulate, so the supply of low-sugar drinks rises from S₁ to S₂, lowering price and raising quantity. Under the UK levy this cut soft drinks' sugar content by 47%. Crucially, sugar intake then falls without consumers buying fewer drinks, so the health gain arrives without the full price burden.

![Figure 3: The market for reformulated low-sugar drinks — because a tiered levy can be avoided by cutting sugar, producers reformulate, supply shifts right from S1 to S2, price falls from P1 to P2 and quantity rises from Q1 to Q2.](diagrams/fig3-reformulation.svg)

## Evaluation

The intervention would improve market outcomes, but four qualifications limit the extent. First, allocative efficiency requires the tax to equal the MEC, which cannot be measured precisely; too high a rate over-corrects and too low a rate leaves the welfare loss intact, so **government failure** is possible, and no reliable estimate of the MEC per litre exists. Second, the sugar association's director Guenter Tissen warns that a "punitive tax" may simply push firms to "replace sugar with artificial sweeteners without improving public health", and that blaming one ingredient is unscientific. This is a genuine **unintended consequence**: the levy corrects the price of sugar in drinks while leaving confectionery, juices and cross-border purchases untaxed, so consumers can substitute and the correction is partial.

Third, the tax is **regressive**, since low-income households spend a larger share of income on SSBs. Their demand is also more elastic, however, so they change consumption most and capture most of the health gain — the burden is arguably progressive in welfare terms. Fourth, the two objectives conflict: if reformulation succeeds the tax base shrinks, so the earmarked revenue funding health initiatives falls exactly when the policy works best.

Politically the measure is feasible — 60% public support, cross-party backing and over 100 precedents — though it restricts **consumer sovereignty**. Overall, a tiered levy would improve market outcomes to a significant extent, but chiefly by changing what producers supply rather than by pricing consumers out. Because the underlying cause is partly information failure, it works best alongside the under-16 ban: young consumers respond weakly to price signals, so direct **regulation** reaches them more effectively than a tax, while education tackles the information failure at its root.
