# Evidence bank — everything you might need, with reliability flagged

## Langston Hughes

**"Mother to Son"** — *The Crisis* (NAACP magazine, ed. W. E. B. Du Bois), December 1922. Hughes was 20. Collected in *The Weary Blues*, 1926. 20 lines, free verse, no stanza breaks.

**Quotations safe to use aloud** (keep them short — the extract is in front of the examiner):
- "Well, son, I'll tell you:"
- "ain't been no crystal stair"
- "tacks," "splinters," "boards torn up"
- "Bare."
- "I'se been a-climbin' on"
- "sometimes goin' in the dark / Where there ain't been no light"
- "So boy, don't you turn back."
- "I'se still goin', honey, / I'se still climbin'"

**Techniques, in order of how impressive they are:**
1. **Same syntax for suffering and survival** — "And splinters" / "And reachin'". Nobody else in your cohort will have spotted this. Lead with it.
2. **"Bare." as the shortest, end-stopped line** — form enacts the missing floorboard.
3. **Present participles throughout** — continuous aspect denies arrival.
4. **Three negative imperatives** (lines 14–17) — hope defined as refusal.
5. **Circular repetition** of line 2 at line 20 — warning becomes credential.
6. **Double negative** "ain't been no" — the poem defines life by subtraction.
7. **"Crystal stair" = mansion + Jacob's ladder** — denies material and spiritual ascent together.
8. **AAVE orthography** — inverts the minstrel-dialect tradition.
9. **Dramatic monologue, no frame narrator** — she is not quoted, she is the poem.

**Context:** Harlem Renaissance. The Great Migration (≈6 million African Americans leaving the rural South from 1916). Jim Crow segregation; the Red Summer riots of 1919. Hughes's essay **"The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain" (1926)** defends writing in Black vernacular — cite it by name if asked about dialect.

**"Dreams" (1922)** — two quatrains. "Hold fast to dreams" (imperative, a verb of grip) / "a broken-winged bird / That cannot fly" / "a barren field / Frozen with snow." Point: the bird is grounded, not killed — hardship ends *movement*.

**"Harlem" (1951)**, from *Montage of a Dream Deferred*. "What happens to a dream deferred?" Chain of similes decaying through the senses: raisin (sight) → sore (wound) → rotten meat (smell) → syrupy sweet (taste) → heavy load (weight) → **"Or does it explode?"** Point: the final line drops the simile and is italicised — it is the only image not held at a distance. Gave Lorraine Hansberry *A Raisin in the Sun* (1959) its title.

## Dorothea Lange

**Confirmed facts:**
- March 1936, pea-pickers' camp, **Nipomo, California**. Late-winter rains had destroyed the pea crop; there was no work.
- Lange was employed by the **Resettlement Administration**, renamed the **Farm Security Administration** in 1937. Photographic unit directed by **Roy Stryker**. Explicit purpose: build public and congressional support for New Deal relief.
- Subject: **Florence Owens Thompson** (1903–1983), **Cherokee**, born in Oklahoma. Aged 32. Photographed with three of her children. Her identity was not public for over 40 years.
- Lange's filed caption: *"Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children. Age thirty-two. Nipomo, California."* She did not record the woman's name.
- Published in a San Francisco newspaper within days; **20,000 pounds of federal food aid** was dispatched to the camp. The family had already moved on.
- The **thumb** on the tent pole at the lower edge of the negative was **retouched out** — by Lange's own decision, roughly two years later.
- Thompson later said Lange never asked her name and that she received nothing for the image.
- Held in the **Library of Congress, FSA/OWI Collection**. Public domain.

**⚠ Contested — do not state flatly:**
- **Number of exposures.** Six negatives survive in the collection; Lange's own 1960 recollection says five. Say both.
- **Which newspaper.** Most sources say the *San Francisco News*; some say the *Chronicle*. Say "a San Francisco paper."

**Visual techniques, in order of how impressive they are:**
1. **Triangular / pyramidal composition = Renaissance Madonna formula.** Lange trained as a studio portraitist — this is knowledge, not accident. The sanctification produces sympathy *and* removes her from politics.
2. **Both older children turn their faces away.** Clears competing eye contact; anonymises them; removes any accusing gaze.
3. **Hand at the chin, partly covering the mouth.** A face of total interiority and no voice. We supply the thought.
4. **Shallow depth of field, background dissolved.** No camp, no employer, no state — the cause is cropped out.
5. **Monochrome** — reads as evidence (documentary code) and abstracts squalor into tone.
6. **Eye-level framing, close** — refuses both pity-from-above and heroism-from-below.
7. **The series moves from wide to tight** — context subtracted in exchange for intensity. This is the argument.
8. **The unlit lantern** in the wider frames — your bridge to Hughes's "where there ain't been no light."
9. **Material detail**: chapped hands, torn sleeve, wedding ring, dirt on the baby.

## The three sentences that carry the whole oral

> Hughes gives his mother a voice and no resolution. Lange gives her mother a face and no voice.

> Hardship and persistence are written in the same grammar — in this poem, surviving does not look different from suffering.

> Resilience is the most beautiful and the most convenient story we can tell about poverty: beautiful because it is true, convenient because admiring someone for surviving costs us nothing.
