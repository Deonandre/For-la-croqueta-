Introduction

Tu poses le sujet et ta thèse. Ne te presse pas : les deux premières phrases donnent le ton de tout l’oral.

“Well, son, I’ll tell you: life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.”

That is a Black mother speaking, in 1922, in her own accent.

Fourteen years later, in a tent in California, another mother — thirty-two years old, seven children, no work — says nothing at all. She is photographed, and one frame from that afternoon becomes the most famous image of American poverty.

My global issue is how art shows poverty, and the limits of individual resilience. My question is not “is poverty hard?” It is this: when art asks us to admire someone for surviving, what does it stop us from looking at?

My literary work is the poetry of Langston Hughes. My extract is “Mother to Son”; “Dreams” and “Harlem” are my wider work. My non-literary work is Dorothea Lange’s photographs at Nipomo, California, in March 1936, made for a United States government agency.

My argument is this. Both artists make poverty easier to look at by showing us a mother who endures. But Hughes warns us that enduring is not enough. Lange, frame by frame, removes the evidence that would let us see why.

Extract 1 — “Mother to Son”

Le plus gros morceau (2 min 40). Six points, dans l’ordre. Si tu te perds, reprends au prochain titre bleu.

1 · The voice

Hughes does not describe this woman. He steps back and lets her speak the whole poem. There is no narrator. The first words — “Well, son, I’ll tell you:” — put us in the middle of a conversation, and the colon tells us a lesson is coming.

And she speaks in Black American dialect: “ain’t,” “I’se been a-climbin’,” “kinder hard.” In 1922 this was risky. Dialect had been used on stage to make Black speech sound funny. Hughes turns that around. Here the dialect is not a sign of ignorance. It is where her authority comes from. She knows because she climbed — and the way she talks is the proof that she climbed.

2 · The staircase

A “crystal stair” is cut glass: a staircase in a rich house. It is also Jacob’s ladder from the Bible — an easy way up, in money and in faith. Hughes refuses both, and he refuses them with a double negative: “ain’t been no crystal stair.” The poem begins by taking something away.

What she gets instead is small and physical: “tacks,” “splinters,” “boards torn up.” These are not big tragedies. They are the injuries of a broken staircase in a bad building. This is ordinary, everyday poverty.

3 · The shape on the page

The lines get shorter and more broken. Line six ends on a dash — “no carpet on the floor—” — and leaves the sentence hanging. Then the poem drops to a single word: “Bare.” One syllable. Full stop. The shortest line in the poem is the moment the floor disappears. The free verse is not decoration. The staircase is the shape of the poem.

4 · The same grammar — your best point

Then the poem turns, but in the same grammar: “And reachin’ landin’s, / And turnin’ corners.”

Hughes lists the obstacles and lists the progress using exactly the same construction. “And splinters.” “And reachin’.” In this poem, surviving does not look different from suffering. It is made of the same material.

And the verbs never finish. Climbin’, reachin’, turnin’, goin’. These are present participles — the “-ing” form. They are continuous. Nothing arrives. Even at the end: “I’se still goin’… I’se still climbin’.” The grammar will not let her reach the top.

5 · The turn at line fourteen

She stops telling and starts commanding: “So boy, don’t you turn back.” Three imperatives, and all three are negative. Don’t turn back. Don’t sit down. Don’t fall. She cannot promise him a destination. She can only forbid three ways of stopping.

Hope in this poem is not optimism. It is refusal.

6 · The last line

The last line repeats line two — almost. Line two is “Life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.” The last line is “And life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.”

That extra word is the whole ending. “And” is the word she used for every stair that hurt her — “And splinters” — and for every step she took anyway — “And reachin’.” So the poem does not close a circle. It adds one more item to the list. The warning comes back as proof, and it comes back as another step.

The rest of Hughes — “Dreams” and “Harlem”

Tu cites, tu ne lis pas. L’idée : Hughes change d’avis entre 1922 et 1951.

“Dreams,” also 1922, starts with a command: “Hold fast.” A verb of gripping. Dreams are not something you have; they are something you can lose hold of. And the punishment is that you stop moving: “a broken-winged bird / That cannot fly.” The bird is not dead. It is grounded. Hardship does not end life here. It ends movement — which is exactly what the staircase was for.

And notice the grammar. In “Dreams” he writes “Life is a broken-winged bird” — a metaphor, stated as fact. In “Harlem” he writes “like a raisin in the sun” — a simile, held at arm’s length. He grows less certain, and the grammar shows it.

Because twenty-nine years later, in “Harlem,” Hughes asks the question “Mother to Son” could not ask. What if you keep climbing and the stairs go nowhere?

“What happens to a dream deferred?” The answers are a chain of similes that rot through the senses: a raisin drying, a sore infecting, meat stinking, a heavy load sagging. Waiting is not neutral. Waiting is decay.

Then the last line breaks the pattern: “Or does it explode?” There is no “like.” Every other image was a simile, held at a safe distance by that one word. This one is not. Hughes sets it alone, in italics, after a blank line. He keeps the determination and takes away the guarantee.

Extract 2 — Lange, Nipomo, March 1936

Montre l’IMAGE A et pointe du doigt chaque élément que tu nommes. C’est là que tu gagnes des points.

1 · Who made it, and why

March 1936. A pea-pickers’ camp at Nipomo, California. The rain had destroyed the crop, so there was no work. The woman is Florence Owens Thompson — thirty-two, Cherokee, born in Oklahoma. Lange filed the picture without her name: “Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children.”

And this is not a neutral record. Lange was paid by the Resettlement Administration, a government agency whose photographers existed to build public support for New Deal spending. These images were made to persuade Congress. And they worked: a San Francisco paper printed one within days, and twenty thousand pounds of federal food were sent to the camp.

2 · Everything that is still in the frame

My photograph is not the famous one. The famous one comes later that afternoon. Mine is the one Lange took on the way there — and it still contains everything she would remove.

On the left, the tent: canvas slanting across half the frame. On the right, a wooden pole holding it up, the bark half stripped off. In the corner, a kerosene lantern and a sack. Behind, past the pole: open field, bare ground, hard daylight.

And the focus is deep. You can read that field — and the field is the economic fact of this picture. It is March, the crop has failed, and the land behind her is producing nothing. This photograph tells you where she is and why she is poor.

3 · Her hands

Her brow is deeply lined and her eyes go past us into the middle distance. But look at her hands: both arms are around the baby. She has no free hand.

Hold on to that, because in the final photograph her hand will come up to her chin — a gesture of thinking, of worry. Here she is not thinking. She is holding.

4 · The girl who looks at us

The older child leans on her shoulder and looks straight out of the picture. Her face is not sweet. It is flat and watchful, and it is aimed at whoever is holding the camera — which means it is aimed at us.

This is the most important thing in my extract, because in the famous photograph nobody looks at us. Lange had a frame in which a child met the viewer’s eye. She did not choose it.

5 · The pole and the lamp

The pole cuts the frame in two: shelter on one side, empty field on the other. It is the strongest line in the picture, and it is barely a worked piece of wood.

And it is the same pole that produced the most revealing edit in the whole series. In the famous photograph, Thompson’s thumb rests on this pole in the bottom corner. Later in the thirties, Lange had that thumb retouched off the negative. The most famous documentary photograph in America is, in the version we all know, edited. In my frame, the pole is whole.

And in the corner there is a lamp, and it is not lit. I will come back to it.

The rest of the series

Montre l’IMAGE B, puis décris la célèbre de mémoire, puis l’IMAGE C.

My other two photographs show what Lange does with this.

[B] Same tent, same pole, same lantern — the same ten minutes. But here she is feeding the baby. Her head is down and her eyes are almost shut. This frame never became famous, and the reason seems clear: a woman nursing a child is too physical and too particular for a newspaper. It is a real mother. It is not a symbol.

Then comes the photograph everyone knows. The tent, the pole, the field and the lantern are all gone. The background is thrown out of focus. Both older children now turn their faces away, so no one looks out. Her hand comes up to her chin. What is left is a shape: her head at the top of a triangle, the children at its base — the standard composition of a Renaissance Madonna, which Lange, a trained portrait photographer, knew well.

So the series moves one way: from a woman to a Madonna. From a body to a symbol. Context removed in exchange for intensity.

[C] And my third photograph shows this is a method, not luck: another migrant mother, somewhere else, holding her children in the same arrangement.

But making her holy also makes her timeless. And a timeless Madonna of suffering is not a woman owed wages by a named employer, in a named county, in 1936. The photograph that moved a nation is the one with the least evidence in it.

Conclusion

Ne presse surtout pas la dernière phrase. Marque un silence avant.

So: Hughes gives his mother a voice and no ending. Lange gives her mother a face and no voice.

And one last thing joins them. In my extract there is a lamp on the ground, and it is not lit. Hughes’s mother says she went “in the dark / Where there ain’t been no light.” Neither of them shows us the top of the stairs.

Both chose a mother, because a mother turns a statistic into a responsibility. You can argue with a number. You cannot argue with a woman holding a baby.

But only one of them admits the limit. Hughes writes it into his own work — “Harlem” is the explosion he placed under “Mother to Son.” Lange’s photographs have no “Harlem.” They offer endurance as the whole answer, and ninety years of reprinting have turned Florence Thompson into a symbol of the strength of people who were, at the time, simply not being paid.

So my global issue is not “people are resilient.” It is that resilience is the most beautiful and the most convenient story we can tell about poverty. Beautiful, because it is true. Convenient, because admiring someone for surviving costs us nothing.

01la plus probableWhy did you choose this frame and not the famous one?

Because the famous one has already decided what to think. My frame still has the tent, the pole, the field and the lantern in it — the things Lange removes over the next few minutes. Choosing the earlier frame lets me show the decision happening, instead of only describing the result.

02Why these two works together?

Because they make the opposite choice. Hughes gives his mother a voice but no ending. Lange gives her mother a face but no voice. Together they show that the limits come from the medium itself, not from a lack of sympathy.

03Is Hughes optimistic?

No. He is determined, which is different. Every promise in the poem is negative: don’t turn back, don’t sit down, don’t fall. He never says the staircase ends. And thirty years later, “Harlem” asks what happens when it doesn’t.

04How can a 1922 poem and a 1951 poem be one work?

Because the work is Hughes’s poetry as a whole, which is normal for a poet. And the gap is the point: it lets me show the same writer testing his own earlier answer.

05You say the photographs are “constructed.” Does that make them dishonest?

No. Every image selects. The question is what the selection costs. Lange’s choices produced real help — twenty thousand pounds of food reached that camp. My point is that the same choices that made the suffering visible made its causes invisible. Both are true at once.

06What does the girl looking at the camera actually do?

It accuses. A direct gaze makes the viewer a participant rather than a spectator, and it is uncomfortable. In the famous frame both children are turned away, so nobody in the picture can hold us responsible. Lange had the accusing version and did not print it.

07What would be lost if the photograph were in colour?

Black and white looks like evidence, so we trust it more. And it turns dirt and torn cloth into tone, which is what lets the image feel dignified instead of ugly. Colour would make her one poor woman in 1936; black and white makes her a symbol — which is exactly my concern.

08Why does each artist choose a mother?

Because a mother turns a statistic into a responsibility. You can argue with a number, but not with a woman holding a baby. It also brings in a second generation, which makes both works about the future of poverty, not only its present.

09Does the dialect limit who the poem can reach?

It narrows the surface and widens the authority. Dialect had been used to make Black speech sound comic, so using it for wisdom is the argument itself. Hughes defended exactly this in his essay “The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain” in 1926.

10Which work is more effective?

Lange is better at producing feeling; Hughes is more honest about what feeling can and cannot do. The photograph got food to a camp in a week; the poem never did anything that measurable. But the photograph also let ninety years of people admire Florence Thompson instead of asking who was underpaying her.

11Why does the retouched thumb matter?

Because it is small. Nobody faked the poverty — Lange just removed a distraction at the edge, on the very pole that holds up the tent in my extract. That is what makes it such a clear example: the image was shaped by ordinary craft decisions, and we received the result as pure truth.

12Is your global issue still relevant today?

It describes almost exactly how we show poverty now. Every charity campaign and every viral photo of someone working three jobs does the same thing: make the person admirable, and the system disappears behind them. Resilience is the version of poverty that is comfortable to look at.

Chaque terme technique que tu vas dire

Dialect / vernaculardialecte
La façon de parler d’un groupe, écrite telle qu’elle se prononce : « ain’t », « I’se », « climbin’ ». Ici, l’anglais afro-américain.
Metaphormétaphore
Dire qu’une chose est une autre, sans « comme ». La vie est un escalier. Elle tient tout le poème, donc : extended metaphor (métaphore filée).
Similecomparaison
Comme la métaphore, mais avec « like » ou « as ». Toutes les images de « Harlem » en sont — sauf la dernière. « Dreams » fait l’inverse : « Life is a broken-winged bird » est une métaphore, affirmée comme un fait.
Double negativedouble négation
« ain’t been no crystal stair » = deux négations dans la même phrase. Normal en dialecte, et ça renforce le refus.
Free versevers libre
Pas de rime, pas de mètre régulier. Les vers ont la longueur que le poète veut — d’où « Bare. » tout seul.
End-stoppedvers arrêté
Un vers qui finit sur un point. « Bare. » est end-stopped : ça bloque net.
Present participleparticipe présent
La forme en « -ing » : climbing, reaching. Action en cours, jamais finie — donc jamais arrivée.
Imperativeimpératif
Un ordre : don’t turn back. Les trois de la fin sont tous négatifs : elle interdit, elle ne promet pas.
Dramatic monologuemonologue dramatique
Un poème entièrement dit par un personnage qui n’est pas le poète. Hughes disparaît ; seule la mère parle.
Compositioncomposition
Où les éléments sont placés dans l’image. Dans la photo célèbre : un triangle, tête en haut, enfants à la base.
Deep / shallow focusprofondeur de champ
Ce qui est net. Ton image A est en deep focus : on lit le champ derrière. La célèbre est en shallow focus : le fond disparaît, et le contexte avec.
Frame / croppingcadre / recadrage
Ce que la photo garde et ce qu’elle coupe. Ton argument porte surtout sur ce qui est coupé entre ton image et la célèbre.
Negativenégatif
La pellicule d’origine, avant tirage. C’est sur le négatif que le pouce a été effacé.

Hughes — les faits

  • “Mother to Son” : publié dans The Crisis (revue de la NAACP) en décembre 1922, Hughes a 20 ans. Repris dans The Weary Blues, 1926. 20 vers, vers libre.
  • Attention : le vers 20 n’est pas identique au vers 2. Vers 2 : « Life for me ain’t been no crystal stair. » Vers 20 : « And life for me ain’t been no crystal stair. » Ne dis jamais « word for word » — l’examinateur a le texte sous les yeux.
  • Repères de vers : « Bare. » = vers 7. Le virage vers les ordres = vers 14. Les trois impératifs négatifs = vers 14, 15, 17.
  • Époque : la Harlem Renaissance ; la Grande Migration (environ 6 millions d’Afro-Américains quittent le Sud rural à partir de 1916) ; la ségrégation Jim Crow.
  • Sur le dialecte, cite son essai “The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain” (1926).
  • “Harlem” (1951), tiré de Montage of a Dream Deferred. A donné son titre à la pièce A Raisin in the Sun (1959).

Lange — les faits

  • Mars 1936, Nipomo, Californie. Les pluies ont détruit la récolte de petits pois : plus de travail.
  • Lange travaille pour la Resettlement Administration (devenue la FSA en 1937), unité photo dirigée par Roy Stryker. But : obtenir le soutien du public et du Congrès pour les aides du New Deal.
  • La femme : Florence Owens Thompson (1903–1983), Cherokee, née en Oklahoma, 32 ans, photographiée avec trois de ses sept enfants. Son nom est resté inconnu plus de 40 ans.
  • Publiée dans un journal de San Francisco en quelques jours ; 20 000 livres (environ 9 tonnes) de nourriture envoyées au camp. La famille était déjà partie.
  • Le pouce posé sur le poteau de tente, en bas à droite de la photo célèbre, a été effacé du négatif (Library of Congress, LC-USF34-009058-C). Le tirage non retouché existe toujours à la LOC.
