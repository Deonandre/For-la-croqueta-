Introduction

Tu poses le sujet et ta thèse. Ne te presse pas : les deux premières phrases donnent le ton de tout l’oral.

“Well, son, I’ll tell you: life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.”

That is a Black mother speaking, in 1922, in her own accent.

Fourteen years later, in a tent in California, another mother — thirty-two years old, seven children, no work — says nothing at all. Her photograph becomes the most famous image of American poverty.

My global issue is how art shows poverty, and the limits of individual resilience.

My question is not “is poverty hard?” My question is this: when art asks us to admire someone for surviving, what does it stop us from looking at?

My literary work is the poetry of Langston Hughes. My extract is “Mother to Son.” I will also use “Dreams” and “Harlem.” My non-literary work is Dorothea Lange’s Migrant Mother series, taken in March 1936 for a United States government agency.

My argument is this. Both artists make poverty easier to look at by showing us a mother who endures. But Hughes warns us that enduring is not enough. Lange does not.

Extract 1 — “Mother to Son”

C’est le plus gros morceau (3 min). Six points, dans l’ordre. Si tu te perds, reprends au prochain titre en bleu.

1 · The voice

Hughes does not describe this woman. He steps back and lets her speak the whole poem. There is no narrator. The first words — “Well, son, I’ll tell you:” — put us in the middle of a conversation. The colon tells us a lesson is coming.

And she speaks in Black American dialect: “ain’t,” “I’se been a-climbin’,” “kinder hard.” In 1922 this was risky. Dialect had been used on stage to make Black speech sound funny. Hughes turns that around. Here the dialect is not a sign of ignorance. It is where her authority comes from. She knows because she climbed — and the way she talks is the proof that she climbed.

2 · The staircase

A “crystal stair” is cut glass. It is a staircase in a rich house. It is also Jacob’s ladder from the Bible — an easy way up, in money and in faith. Hughes refuses both. And he refuses them with a double negative: “ain’t been no crystal stair.” The poem begins by taking something away.

What she gets instead is small and physical: “tacks,” “splinters,” “boards torn up.” These are not big tragedies. They are the injuries of a broken staircase in a bad building. This is ordinary, everyday poverty.

3 · The shape on the page

The lines get shorter and more broken. Line six ends on a dash — “no carpet on the floor—” — and leaves the sentence hanging. Then the poem drops to a single word: “Bare.” One syllable. Full stop. The shortest line in the poem is the moment the floor disappears. The free verse is not decoration. The staircase is the shape of the poem.

4 · The same grammar — your best point

Then the poem turns, but in the same grammar: “And reachin’ landin’s, / And turnin’ corners.”

Hughes lists the obstacles and lists the progress using exactly the same construction. “And splinters.” “And reachin’.” The suffering and the surviving are built the same way. In this poem, surviving does not look different from suffering. It is made of the same material.

And the verbs never finish. Climbin’, reachin’, turnin’, goin’. These are present participles — the “-ing” form. They are continuous. Nothing arrives. Even at the end: “I’se still goin’… I’se still climbin’.” The grammar will not let her reach the top.

5 · The turn at line fourteen

She stops telling and starts commanding: “So boy, don’t you turn back.”

Three imperatives, and all three are negative. Don’t turn back. Don’t sit down. Don’t fall. She cannot promise him a destination. She can only forbid three ways of stopping.

This is the key point for my global issue. Hope in this poem is not optimism. It is refusal.

6 · The last line

The last line repeats line two — almost. Line two is “Life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.” The last line is “And life for me ain’t been no crystal stair.”

That extra word is the whole ending. “And” is the word she used for every stair that hurt her — “And splinters,” “And boards torn up” — and for every step she took anyway — “And reachin’,” “And turnin’.” So the poem does not close a circle. It adds one more item to the list. The warning comes back as proof, and it comes back as another step.

The rest of Hughes — “Dreams” and “Harlem”

Ici tu montres que tu connais l’œuvre entière, pas seulement l’extrait. L’idée : Hughes change d’avis entre 1922 et 1951.

Hughes keeps asking this question across his work.

“Dreams,” also 1922, starts with a command: “Hold fast.” That is a verb of gripping. Dreams are not something you have. They are something you can lose hold of. And the punishment is that you stop moving: “a broken-winged bird / That cannot fly.” The bird is not dead. It is grounded. Hardship does not end life here. It ends movement — which is exactly what the staircase was for.

And notice the grammar. In “Dreams” he writes “Life is a broken-winged bird” — a metaphor, stated as fact. In “Harlem” he writes “like a raisin in the sun” — a simile, held at arm’s length. He grows less certain, and the grammar shows it.

But twenty-nine years later, in “Harlem,” Hughes asks the question “Mother to Son” could not ask. What if you keep climbing and the stairs go nowhere?

“What happens to a dream deferred?” The answers are a chain of similes that rot through the senses: a raisin drying, a sore infecting, meat stinking, sugar crusting over, a heavy load sagging. Waiting is not neutral. Waiting is decay.

Then the last line breaks the pattern: “Or does it explode?” There is no “like.” Every other image was a simile, held at a safe distance by that one word. This one is not. Hughes also sets it alone, in italics, after a blank line. The dream stops being something to describe and becomes something that detonates.

So Hughes does not say: endure, and you will be fine. He says: this woman endured, and her grandchildren may not. He keeps the determination. He takes away the guarantee.

Extract 2 — Lange, Migrant Mother

Montre la feuille à l’examinateur et pointe du doigt ce que tu décris. Cinq points.

1 · Who made it, and why

March 1936. A pea-pickers’ camp in Nipomo, California. The rain had destroyed the crop, so there was no work. The woman is Florence Owens Thompson — thirty-two, Cherokee, born in Oklahoma. Lange filed the photo without her name: “Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children.”

The first thing to say is that this is not a neutral record. Lange was paid by the Resettlement Administration — a government agency whose photographers existed to build public support for New Deal spending. This image was made to persuade Congress. And it worked. A San Francisco paper printed it within days, and twenty thousand pounds of federal food were sent to the camp.

2 · The triangle

The mother’s head is at the top of a triangle. The two older children lean in on each side and make the base. This triangle is not an accident. It is the standard shape of a Renaissance painting of the Madonna and child. Lange trained as a portrait photographer. She knew this. The effect is instant sympathy: a farm worker is given the visual language of a religious painting.

But this is where I want to push. Making her holy also makes her timeless. And a timeless Madonna of suffering is not a woman who is owed wages, by a named employer, in a named county, in 1936. The thing that creates our sympathy also lifts her out of politics.

3 · The children

Both of them turn their faces into her shoulders. This does two things. It removes every other point of eye contact, so all our attention goes to the mother. And it makes the children anonymous — any children, every child. The cost is that nobody in this photograph is allowed to look at us and accuse us.

4 · The face

Her hand is at her chin. It looks like worry, like calculation, like patience. It also partly covers her mouth. Her eyes go past the camera into the distance. Lange gives her a face full of thought, and gives her no voice. We are invited to imagine what she is thinking — which means we invent it ourselves. The power of the photograph is that it is an empty space we fill with our own feelings.

5 · What is missing

The background is out of focus. The tent canvas becomes soft grey. There is no camp, no other families, no field, no employer, no government. The photograph makes suffering very visible and makes the cause of it invisible. It cuts out everything you could blame.

One last detail, and I think it is the sharpest. In the original negative, her thumb is visible at the bottom of the frame. Lange had it removed. The most famous documentary photograph in America is, in the version we all know, edited. And Thompson said later that Lange never asked her name, and that she was paid nothing.

The rest of the series

Pointe la photo de gauche (plan large), puis celle de droite (plan serré). C’est là que les deux images servent vraiment.

The rest of the series proves this was built, not found — because we can watch Lange decide.

In the earlier frames, including the second image I have brought, you can see the whole tent. There is an unlit lantern on the ground. There is open field behind. And the older daughter looks straight into the camera. These are documentary in the normal sense: they tell you where this family is and what they own.

Then, shot by shot, Lange moves closer. The field goes. The lantern goes. The girl’s look goes. By the last frame, only faces are left. The series records a decision: she removes the context to gain the intensity.

I want to keep the lantern, though, because it answers Hughes. It is a lamp, and it is not lit. Hughes’s mother says she went “in the dark / Where there ain’t been no light.” Lange photographs the unlit lamp at the edge of the frame. Neither of them shows us the top of the stairs.

Conclusion

Ne presse surtout pas la dernière phrase. Marque un silence avant.

So: Hughes gives his mother a voice and no ending. Lange gives her mother a face and no voice. His poem ends on a line that refuses to arrive. Her photograph ends on a look that refuses us.

Both chose a mother, because a mother turns a statistic into a responsibility. You can argue with a number. You cannot argue with a woman holding a baby.

But only one of them admits the limit. Hughes writes it into his own work — “Harlem” is the explosion he placed under “Mother to Son.” Lange’s photograph has no “Harlem.” It offers endurance as the whole answer. And ninety years of reprinting have turned Florence Thompson into a symbol of the strength of people who were, at the time, simply not being paid.

So my global issue is not “people are resilient.” It is that resilience is the most beautiful and the most convenient story we can tell about poverty. Beautiful, because it is true. Convenient, because admiring someone for surviving costs us nothing.

Après tes 10 minutes, l’examinateur pose des questions pendant 5 minutes. Voici les plus probables. Apprends l’idée, pas les mots exacts. Termine toujours en revenant à ton global issue.

01Why these two works together?

Because they make the opposite choice. Hughes gives his mother a voice but no ending. Lange gives her mother a face but no voice. Putting them together shows that the limits come from the medium itself, not from a lack of sympathy.

02Is Hughes optimistic?

No. He is determined, which is different. Every promise in the poem is negative: don’t turn back, don’t sit down, don’t fall. He never says the staircase ends. And thirty years later, “Harlem” asks what happens when it doesn’t.

03How can a 1922 poem and a 1951 poem be one work?

Because the work is Hughes’s poetry as a whole, which is normal for a poet. And the gap is the point. It lets me show the same writer testing his own earlier answer.

04You say the photograph is “constructed.” Does that make it dishonest?

No. Every image selects. The question is what the selection costs. Lange’s choices produced real help — twenty thousand pounds of food reached that camp. My point is that the same choices that made the suffering visible made its causes invisible. Both things are true at once.

05What would be lost if the photograph were in colour?

Two things. Black and white looks like evidence, so we trust it more. And it turns dirt and torn cloth into tone, which is what lets the image feel sacred instead of ugly. Colour would make her one poor woman in 1936. Black and white makes her a symbol — which is exactly my concern.

06Why does each artist choose a mother?

Because a mother turns a statistic into a responsibility. You can argue with a number, but not with a woman holding a baby. It also brings in a second generation, which makes both works about the future of poverty, not only its present.

07Does the dialect limit who the poem can reach?

It narrows the surface and widens the authority. Dialect had been used to make Black speech sound comic, so using it for wisdom is the argument itself. Hughes defended exactly this in his essay “The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain” in 1926.

08Which work is more effective?

Lange is better at producing feeling. Hughes is more honest about what feeling can and cannot do. The photograph got food to a camp in a week; the poem never did anything that measurable. But the photograph also let ninety years of people admire Florence Thompson instead of asking who was underpaying her.

09Is your global issue still relevant today?

It describes almost exactly how we show poverty now. Every charity campaign and every viral photo of someone working three jobs does the same thing: make the person admirable, and the system disappears behind them. Resilience is the version of poverty that is comfortable to look at.

10What is the effect of the children turning away?

It clears every other point of eye contact, so the mother carries all our attention. It makes the children anonymous. And it means nobody in the frame is allowed to look at us and accuse us — which is a large part of why the image is bearable.

11Why does the retouched thumb matter?

Because it is small. Nobody faked the poverty — Lange just removed a distraction at the edge. That is what makes it such a clear example: the image was shaped by ordinary craft decisions, and we received the result as pure truth.

12Could you have chosen a different Hughes poem?

“Harlem” would work, but it would flip the oral — I would be analysing the failure of endurance instead of its cost. “Mother to Son” is better because it is the version that still believes, and that lets me measure how far Hughes moves by 1951.

Chaque terme technique que tu vas dire

Dialect / vernaculardialecte
La façon de parler d’un groupe, écrite telle qu’elle se prononce : « ain’t », « I’se », « climbin’ ». Ici c’est l’anglais afro-américain.
Metaphormétaphore
Dire qu’une chose est une autre, sans « comme ». La vie est un escalier. Elle tient tout le poème, donc on dit extended metaphor (métaphore filée).
Similecomparaison
Comme la métaphore, mais avec « like » ou « as ». Toutes les images de « Harlem » en sont — sauf la dernière. « Dreams » fait l’inverse : « Life is a broken-winged bird » est une métaphore, affirmée comme un fait.
Double negativedouble négation
« ain’t been no crystal stair » = deux négations dans la même phrase. Normal en dialecte, et ça renforce le refus.
Free versevers libre
Pas de rime, pas de mètre régulier. Les vers ont donc la longueur que le poète veut — d’où « Bare. » tout seul.
End-stoppedvers arrêté
Un vers qui finit sur un point. « Bare. » est end-stopped : ça bloque net.
Present participleparticipe présent
La forme en « -ing » : climbing, reaching. Action en cours, jamais finie — donc jamais arrivée.
Imperativeimpératif
Un ordre : don’t turn back. Les trois de la fin sont tous négatifs : elle interdit, elle ne promet pas.
Dramatic monologuemonologue dramatique
Un poème entièrement dit par un personnage qui n’est pas le poète. Hughes disparaît ; seule la mère parle.
Compositioncomposition
Où les éléments sont placés dans l’image. Ici : un triangle, tête en haut, enfants à la base.
Depth of fieldprofondeur de champ
Ce qui est net et ce qui est flou. Ici elle est shallow (faible) : le fond disparaît, donc le contexte aussi.
Frame / croppingcadre / recadrage
Ce que la photo garde et ce qu’elle coupe. Ton argument porte surtout sur ce qui est coupé.
Negativenégatif
La pellicule d’origine, avant tirage. C’est là que le pouce a été retouché.

Hughes — les faits

  • “Mother to Son” : publié dans The Crisis (revue de la NAACP) en décembre 1922, Hughes a 20 ans. Repris dans The Weary Blues, 1926. 20 vers, vers libre.
  • Attention : le vers 20 n’est pas identique au vers 2. Vers 2 : « Life for me ain’t been no crystal stair. » Vers 20 : « And life for me ain’t been no crystal stair. » Ne dis jamais « word for word » — l’examinateur a le texte sous les yeux. Ce « And » en plus est un meilleur argument de toute façon.
  • Repères de vers : « Bare. » = vers 7. Le virage vers les ordres = vers 14. Les trois impératifs négatifs = vers 14, 15, 17.
  • Époque : la Harlem Renaissance ; la Grande Migration (environ 6 millions d’Afro-Américains quittent le Sud rural à partir de 1916) ; la ségrégation Jim Crow.
  • Si on te parle du dialecte, cite son essai “The Negro Artist and the Racial Mountain” (1926) : il y défend exactement ce choix.
  • “Dreams” (1922) : « Hold fast », un verbe de prise. L’oiseau n’est pas mort, il est cloué au sol.
  • “Harlem” (1951), tiré de Montage of a Dream Deferred. A donné son titre à la pièce A Raisin in the Sun (1959).

Lange — les faits

  • Mars 1936, Nipomo, Californie. Les pluies ont détruit la récolte de petits pois : plus de travail.
  • Lange travaille pour la Resettlement Administration (devenue la FSA en 1937), dirigée par Roy Stryker. Objectif de l’unité photo : obtenir le soutien du public et du Congrès pour les aides du New Deal.
  • La femme : Florence Owens Thompson (1903–1983), Cherokee, née en Oklahoma, 32 ans, photographiée avec trois de ses sept enfants. Son nom est resté inconnu plus de 40 ans.
  • Publiée dans un journal de San Francisco en quelques jours ; 20 000 livres (environ 9 tonnes) de nourriture envoyées au camp. La famille était déjà partie.
  • Le pouce visible sur le poteau de tente a été retouché sur le négatif, sur décision de Lange, environ deux ans plus tard.
  • Conservée à la Library of Congress (collection FSA/OWI), domaine public.
La légende écrite par Lange :
“Destitute pea pickers in California. Mother of seven children. Age thirty-two. Nipomo, California.”

Le nombre de prises. Six négatifs subsistent ; Lange elle-même en a dit cinq en 1960. Dis les deux.
