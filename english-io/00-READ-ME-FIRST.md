## Diagnosis of the draft you brought

It was competently written and would have passed. It would not have scored well, for five reasons.

**1. It was roughly 17 minutes long.** The draft ran ~2,400 words. A 10-minute oral is ~1,450. You would have been stopped mid-section, which damages Criterion C badly.

**2. The global issue was a topic, not an issue.** *"The impact of hardship on individuals and their determination to overcome challenges"* cannot be argued against, so there was nothing for Criterion B to reward. The sharpened version carries a built-in tension you can spend ten minutes resolving.

**3. The analysis was mostly paraphrase.** The draft restated what the texts say and then named a device. Criterion B asks what the **choices** do. I replaced the paraphrase with the technical features that were missing entirely:

- the poem is in **African American Vernacular English**, and in 1922 that was a political act against the minstrel-dialect tradition — the draft never mentioned dialect at all
- **"Bare."** — the shortest line in the poem, where the form drops out from under you
- the **identical grammar** used for the obstacles and for the progress — the strongest single observation available in this poem
- the **present participles** that deny arrival
- the **circular repetition** of line 2 at line 20
- Lange's **triangular Madonna composition**, which is the whole reason the image works
- the **Resettlement Administration** commission — these are government persuasion images, not neutral records
- the **retouched thumb**

**4. It treated the photograph as found, not made.** The draft said the mother "appears" thoughtful and that resilience is "something the viewer may infer." True, but passive. Once you know Lange was shooting for a New Deal agency and moved closer frame by frame, the series becomes evidence of decisions you can analyse — which is where the marks are.

**5. There was no argument.** The draft listed similarities and differences and concluded that both works "suggest hardship affects people." The new version argues something that could be wrong, and defends it: *both artists make poverty bearable by routing it through a mother's endurance, but Hughes builds a warning into his own form and Lange does not.*

## What I kept
Your structure (literary → wider work → non-literary → wider body → comparison) is correct and I kept it. Your instinct that both works centre a **mother** is the right comparative hinge — I only sharpened it into *why*. And your closing caution that determination doesn't remove the barriers is genuinely good; it is now the thesis rather than an afterthought.

## Three practical things before the exam

1. **Print two Lange frames, not one** — the wide frame you already have *and* the canonical tight portrait, side by side on one sheet. See `02-EXTRACTS.md`. This single change upgrades your Criterion B more than anything else here.
2. **Time yourself out loud, three times.** Not in your head — out loud, with a timer. Note where you are at 5:00. You should be opening the Lange section.
3. **If you already registered the old global-issue wording with your teacher, keep the paperwork as it is** and simply speak the sharpened version. Examiners mark what you say.

## Honest caveats

- **Two factual points are genuinely contested** in the sources and are flagged in `04-EVIDENCE-BANK.md`: how many exposures Lange made (six survive; she recalled five) and which San Francisco paper ran it. Say both versions. Knowing a source is disputed reads as better scholarship than stating one number confidently.
- I have **not** seen your teacher's mark scheme or any class-specific requirements. If your school requires a particular extract length, a written outline, or a fixed global-issue form, follow that over this.
- The script is written to be **spoken by you**. Change any phrase that doesn't sound like your voice — an oral delivered in someone else's register is worse than a plainer one in your own.
