## 1. Introduction and environmental context

Deforestation is one of the clearest cases of human activity altering an environmental system faster
than that system can recover. Removing closed-canopy tropical forest reduces at once the carbon held
in biomass, the habitat supporting high species richness, the regulation of local rainfall and the
binding of soil against erosion. Forest is a store of natural capital and the services it provides
are natural income. Clearing it replaces a largely self-regulating system, held near a steady state
by internal negative feedback, with a simplified agricultural system that stays productive only
through continuous human input. Recovery is slow: regrowth may restore canopy within decades but not
the species composition or soil structure of primary forest.

Cameroon lies at the north-western edge of the Congo Basin, the world's second largest block of
tropical rainforest. Clearing forest releases the carbon stored in trees and soil, so deforestation
appears in national greenhouse-gas accounts as CO₂ emissions from land-use change. For Cameroon this
flow is not a minor category: in {{LUC_MAX_YR}} it reached {{LUC_MAX}} Mt CO₂, and even in 2023 it
was {{LUC_2023}} Mt, roughly {{LUC_VS_FOSSIL}} times the country's fossil-fuel emissions of
{{FOSSIL_2023}} Mt. Cameroon's climate impact is therefore mainly a land-use story rather than an
energy one, which is typical of forested low-income countries and makes land-use-change emissions a
useful single measure of how heavily forests are being converted.

What drives that conversion is contested. Tyukavina et al. (2018) found that forest disturbance
across the Congo Basin between 2000 and 2014 was dominated by small-scale, largely manual clearing
for agriculture rather than by industrial logging, and that the annual rate of this clearing almost
doubled. Because smallholder clearing is carried out by households, the common explanation is
population pressure: more people require more farmland, and farmland comes from forest. Cocoa, the
region's leading export tree crop, is often named as the driver — Ordway et al. (2017) identified
cocoa as the fastest-expanding export crop in sub-Saharan Africa, with most expansion occurring
outside industrial plantations. The population-pressure argument is intuitive and widely repeated,
but it is an empirical claim, and it can be tested against national data rather than assumed.

## 2. Research question and rationale

**To what extent is population growth associated with CO₂ emissions from land-use change in Cameroon
between 1961 and 2023?**

The question matters because the answer shapes policy. If forest loss tracks population, the
response is to raise agricultural yields so that a growing population needs less new land. If it
does not, attention belongs instead with export markets, roads, logging and governance, and blaming
population growth misdirects effort onto the households with the least capacity to change.

**Independent variable:** the annual increase in Cameroon's population (people per year).
**Dependent variable:** annual CO₂ emissions from land-use change in Cameroon (million tonnes,
Mt CO₂ per year).
**Controlled aspects:** one country, one dataset version, identical calendar years for both
variables, and the same statistical test throughout.

Population is used as a flow rather than a level for a specific reason. Total population is a
*stock* — the number of people alive in a given year — whereas land-use-change emissions are a
*flow*, the carbon released during that year. Correlating a stock against a flow across six decades
would mostly measure whether two series happen to trend together over time. Defining population
growth as the **annual increase** puts both variables on a per-year basis, which is the defensible
comparison. The levels correlation is still reported, but as a check on that reasoning rather than
as the main result.

**Boundaries.** Cameroon is the spatial unit because both variables are reported to consistent
national boundaries. The period begins in 1961, the first year after independence and the start of
continuous modern statistics, and ends in 2023, the last complete year in the emissions dataset.

## 3. Strategy and perspectives

The issue sits inside a genuine conflict, and the groups involved hold different environmental value
systems that lead them to read the same forest differently.

**Smallholder farmers.** For rural households, forest is a land reserve and cash crops are one of
the few reliable sources of income. Clearing a hectare is rational for a household with limited
capital: forest soils are fertile, the land is often effectively free to occupy, and a cocoa or oil
palm plot yields income for decades. This anthropocentric position is not an uninformed one — it
reflects real constraints rather than indifference to forests.

**The national economy.** Agricultural exports earn foreign exchange and employ a large share of the
workforce, so the state has an interest in sustained output. This creates a tension within government,
since the same state holds forest-protection commitments. Restricting expansion carries a measurable
economic cost, which is why forest policy is often weakly enforced.

**Conservation interests.** From an ecocentric standpoint, Congo Basin forest has value as habitat,
as a carbon store and as a regulator of regional hydrology — value largely non-monetised and
accruing globally rather than locally. That asymmetry is central: the benefits of clearing are
captured locally and immediately, while the costs of lost ecosystem services are global and
deferred. This externality explains why the issue is not resolved by farmers simply choosing better.

**Where responsibility is placed.** A population-pressure framing is itself a perspective with
consequences. It locates the cause in the fertility of poor rural households rather than in
consumption elsewhere, and it has historically been used to justify coercive policy. Testing the
claim rather than assuming it is therefore an ethical question as well as a scientific one.

Three strategies are advanced, each with real drawbacks.

**Agroforestry.** Growing cocoa under shade retains part of the canopy, supports more biodiversity
than full-sun monoculture, stores more carbon and protects soil. It is already the dominant system
in Cameroon. Its weakness is yield: shaded cocoa produces less per hectare, so if demand is fixed,
lower yields require *more* land. This is the land-sharing versus land-sparing tension. Gockowski and
Sonwa (2011) modelled the alternative and estimated that if intensified cocoa technology had been
adopted from the 1960s across Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Nigeria and Cameroon, more than 21,000 km² of
deforestation and degradation and roughly 1.4 billion tonnes of CO₂ emissions could have been
avoided. Their conclusion is uncomfortable for conservation thinking: intensification, though it
reduces on-farm biodiversity, may spare more forest overall. Which strategy is "better" depends on
whether value is placed on biodiversity within the farm or on total forest left standing.

**Certification.** Schemes such as Rainforest Alliance and Fairtrade reward deforestation-free
production with price premiums. They give consumers leverage and can transfer income, but depend on
audit quality, cover only part of production, and are vulnerable to leakage: if certified farms stop
expanding while demand continues, clearing moves elsewhere.

**Regulation.** The EU Deforestation Regulation (European Union, 2023) requires importers to show
that commodities including cocoa were not grown on land deforested after 2020. It is the strongest
instrument available, but places the compliance burden on smallholders least able to carry it,
raising a question of equity: who pays to protect a global public good?

These perspectives shape outcomes directly: because the most economically rational household choice
removes forest, and the resulting costs fall largely outside Cameroon, forest loss continues even
where all parties agree it is undesirable.

## 4. Methodology

This investigation uses **exclusively secondary quantitative data**: no fieldwork, interviews,
questionnaires or experiments were involved. It is a reproducible desk-based correlational analysis.

**Source.** All variables come from the CO₂ and Greenhouse Gas Emissions dataset published by Our
World in Data, which distributes a single compiled file through its public repository. The
underlying sources are the Global Carbon Budget (Friedlingstein et al., 2025) for emissions and the
Maddison Project Database (Bolt & van Zanden, 2025) for GDP.

**Selection.** An agricultural land-area variable would have matched the research question more
directly, so World Bank *agricultural land* and *permanent cropland* series for Cameroon were
examined first and **rejected**. In those series 37% and 46% of years respectively showed exactly
zero change from the previous year, and permanent cropland was identical in all eight years from
2011 to 2018. Values repeated to that degree are carried forward rather than measured, and
correlating them would produce a coefficient describing a reporting convention rather than the
environment. Land-use-change CO₂ was used instead: it has {{N_PRIMARY}} usable years with no
repeated consecutive values.

**Extraction and processing.** The compiled file was downloaded, filtered to Cameroon and to
1961–2023, and the four columns above were retained. Annual population increase was computed as
ΔPₜ = Pₜ − Pₜ₋₁, which necessarily loses the first year. Land-use-change emissions per person were
computed to separate total from per-head intensity. All steps are scripted, so every figure in this
report traces back to the unmodified download.

**Statistical treatment.** **Spearman's rank correlation (rₛ)** is the primary test. Shapiro–Wilk
tests reject normality for both land-use-change emissions (*p* = {{SW_LUC_P}}) and annual population
increase (*p* = {{SW_POP_P}}), so Pearson's assumption of bivariate normality does not hold; Spearman
requires only a monotonic relationship and resists outliers. Pearson's *r* is reported alongside for
comparison. For every coefficient the sample size, two-tailed *p*-value and the critical value at
α = 0.05 are given.

Four further tests address specific threats to validity:

1. **Levels comparison** — total population against emissions, to show how much apparent association
   comes from shared time trend.
2. **First differences** — both variables differenced, removing any common trend.
3. **Lagged correlation (0–6 years)** — clearing may precede the emissions accounting, and new
   farmland may be cleared in anticipation of population growth.
4. **Sub-period split at 1990** — to test whether one relationship holds across six decades of very
   different economic conditions.

## 6. Analysis and discussion

**Trends.** Between 1961 and 2023 Cameroon's population rose from {{POP_1961}} million to
{{POP_2023}} million, a factor of {{POP_FACTOR}}, increasing in every single year. Land-use-change
emissions did not follow. They moved from {{LUC_1961}} Mt to {{LUC_2023}} Mt, a change of
{{LUC_PCT}}%, fluctuating around a mean of {{LUC_MEAN}} Mt with a standard deviation of {{LUC_SD}}
Mt and no steady direction: decade means fell from {{DEC_1970S}} Mt in the 1970s to {{DEC_1990S}} Mt
in the 1990s, then rose again to {{DEC_2000S}} Mt in the 2000s. One variable grows monotonically;
the other oscillates. That alone makes a strong positive association unlikely.

**Primary result.** Annual population increase against land-use-change emissions gives
rₛ = {{RHO_PRIMARY}} (*p* = {{P_PRIMARY}}, *n* = {{N_PRIMARY}}, critical |r| = {{CRIT_PRIMARY}}) —
a {{STRENGTH}} and {{SIG_WORD}} association, but **negative**. Years of faster population growth
were, if anything, years of *lower* deforestation emissions. The levels comparison agrees
(rₛ = {{RHO_LEVELS}}, *p* = {{P_LEVELS}}). Pearson's *r* = {{R_PRIMARY}} (*p* = {{PP_PRIMARY}}) is
much weaker and not significant. The two tests disagreeing is informative rather than awkward: it
indicates a relationship that is monotonic in rank but not linear, with extreme years — including an
outlier of {{LUC_MAX}} Mt in {{LUC_MAX_YR}} — pulling the least-squares line while barely affecting
the ranks. Reporting Pearson alone would have concealed the pattern; reporting it alone as
significant would have overstated it.

**The association does not survive detrending.** With both variables expressed as year-on-year
changes, rₛ falls to {{RHO_DIFFS}} (*p* = {{P_DIFFS}}, *n* = {{N_DIFFS}}) — {{DIFFS_SIG_WORD}}, and
essentially zero. This is the single most important result. It shows that the negative coefficient
is a property of the two long-run trajectories, not of any year-to-year link: population rose
steadily while emissions drifted down, so ranking them against each other produces a negative
coefficient even though a year of unusually fast population growth tells us nothing about that
year's emissions. The lag tests point the same way, with the strongest coefficient at lag
{{BEST_LAG}} ({{BEST_LAG_RHO}}) and no peak at any plausible delay.

**The relationship reverses between sub-periods.** For 1961–1990, rₛ = {{RHO_EARLY}}
(*p* = {{P_EARLY}}, *n* = {{N_EARLY}}); for 1991–2023 it is {{RHO_LATE}} (*p* = {{P_LATE}},
*n* = {{N_LATE}}). The sign flips. A single coefficient for the whole period therefore averages two
opposite patterns, and the headline answer depends on where the period boundaries are drawn — a
reminder that a correlation computed over an arbitrary span can be an artefact of that span.

**Decoupling.** The clearest finding is in intensity. Land-use-change emissions per person fell from
{{PC_1961}} t CO₂ in 1961 to {{PC_2023}} t in 2023, a decline of {{PC_DROP}}%. Cameroon added
roughly {{POP_FACTOR}} times its 1961 population while the carbon released per head from land
conversion fell by an order of magnitude. This is relative decoupling: forest is still being
converted, but far less per additional person. Figure 6 shows the same downward path across the
region, so this is not unique to Cameroon.

**Why population growth may not drive clearing proportionally.** Several mechanisms are consistent
with the data. Much population growth has been absorbed by cities rather than by new farms, so
additional people do not translate into proportionally more cleared land. Yields on existing land
have risen. The most accessible forest near roads and settlements was converted early, so later
clearing faces higher costs. Economic conditions matter independently of population: the collapse in
commodity prices and the structural adjustment period of the late 1980s and 1990s coincide with the
lowest emissions decade in the series, which suggests that the ability to invest in new plantations
constrains clearing more than the number of people does. Consistent with this, GDP per capita also
correlates negatively with emissions (rₛ = {{RHO_GDPPC}}, *p* = {{P_GDPPC}}), so affluence is not
substituting for population as an explanation either.

**Alternative explanations and limits.** Land-use-change emissions aggregate every cause of carbon
loss from land: smallholder clearing, logging, oil palm, fuelwood, fire and peat, minus regrowth
uptake. A fall could reflect less clearing, or clearing of land holding less carbon, or stronger
regrowth. These cannot be separated here. Crucially, this variable is **modelled rather than
measured**, so part of its year-to-year movement reflects model assumptions. For all these reasons
**no causal claim is made**: the analysis shows that population growth and deforestation emissions
in Cameroon have not moved together, not that population growth has no effect on forests.

## 7. Conclusion

Answering the research question directly: population growth is associated with CO₂ emissions from
land-use change in Cameroon between 1961 and 2023 only to a {{STRENGTH}} extent, and in the
**opposite direction** to the population-pressure hypothesis. The primary test gives
rₛ = {{RHO_PRIMARY}} (*p* = {{P_PRIMARY}}, *n* = {{N_PRIMARY}}), which is {{SIG_WORD}} and negative.
That association does not survive detrending — first differences give rₛ = {{RHO_DIFFS}}
(*p* = {{P_DIFFS}}), {{DIFFS_SIG_WORD}} — and it reverses sign between the two sub-periods. The
defensible conclusion is that at national scale there is **no stable year-to-year relationship**
between how fast Cameroon's population grew and how much carbon its land-use change released, while
emissions per person fell by {{PC_DROP}}%.

The objectives were largely achieved: two complete annual series were assembled, tested with
appropriate non-parametric statistics, and checked against three separate threats to validity. The
investigation did not, and at national resolution could not, identify what does drive Cameroon's
deforestation. Every statement here rests on the data presented.

## 8. Evaluation

**Reliability of the data.** The Global Carbon Budget is peer-reviewed, revised annually and
transparently documented, and population coverage is complete for every year. The serious
qualification is that land-use-change emissions are **output of bookkeeping models**, not
measurements. They combine land-use statistics with assumed carbon densities, and this term carries
the largest uncertainty in the entire global carbon budget. Treating these figures as observations
would overstate their precision, and because estimates are revised when the models change, a later
release could alter the correlations reported here. GDP is also missing for 2023.

**Validity.** Land-use-change CO₂ is a proxy for deforestation, not a measure of it. It is a *net*
flux that includes degradation and peat and subtracts regrowth uptake, and it depends on the carbon
density of the land cleared. A fall in emissions is therefore consistent with less clearing, with
clearing of lower-carbon land, or with greater regrowth — three environmentally different outcomes
that the data cannot distinguish. The independent variable is also crude: **total** population
counts urban residents, whose land demand differs completely from that of farming households. Rural
population would have been the better variable and is the clearest single improvement available.

**Limitations of national data.** Aggregating to the national total averages the forested south,
where clearing occurs, with the drier north, where little forest remains. Inferring household
behaviour from national aggregates risks the ecological fallacy: a national correlation does not
describe what individual families do.

**Statistical limitations.** With *n* = {{N_PRIMARY}}, a coefficient must exceed
{{CRIT_PRIMARY}} to reach significance, so only a fairly strong relationship is detectable.
More importantly, thirteen correlations were computed; at α = 0.05 roughly one in twenty would be
expected to appear significant by chance, so marginal results such as the 1991–2023 sub-period
(*p* = {{P_LATE}}) should not be read as findings. Correlation cannot establish causal direction or
exclude a third variable. The sign reversal between sub-periods shows directly that the whole-period
coefficient is unstable.

**Confounding variables** not controlled include urbanisation rate, world commodity prices,
structural adjustment and debt, oil revenue, timber concession activity, road construction, fuelwood
demand and conflict-driven displacement — several of which plausibly affect both variables.

**Improvements.** Substituting rural for total population would sharpen the test immediately.
Repeating the analysis with a directly observed measure of forest area — FAO Forest Resources
Assessment figures or satellite tree-cover-loss data — would remove dependence on modelled
emissions. Working at regional rather than national scale would remove most of the aggregation
problem. A multiple regression including population, income and commodity prices would begin to
separate drivers that correlation cannot.

**Further research.** The attribution question is better answered by spatial overlay than by
national correlation: combining district-level population with satellite forest-loss maps would show
directly whether clearing happens where people are increasing. Comparing Cameroon with Côte d'Ivoire
and Ghana, where cocoa is mainly full-sun and forest loss has been heavier, would test whether
shaded cultivation genuinely reduces forest loss per tonne produced.
