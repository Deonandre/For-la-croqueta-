# 1. Introduction

A philosophy attributed to any prominent individual never reaches the audience directly. It arrives inside an interview, a broadcast, a book or a film, and each of those media sets limits on what can be communicated and how. Kobe Bryant is a good example, because the phrase attached to him has outlived both his career and his life. “Mamba Mentality” is now known well beyond basketball, as a general name for disciplined hard work. Bryant supplied the phrase and almost all of its content. He did not create the form in which it is communicated.

The following analysis of two different texts includes three interviews: the profile by Chuck Klosterman in *GQ* in February 2015, Bryant’s participation in *The Grantland Basketball Hour* with Bill Simmons and Jalen Rose, also in February 2015, and press conference where he announced his retirement on 29 November 2015. The second kind of text is the movie *Kobe Bryant’s Muse* directed by Gotham Chopra, which had its premiere on Showtime on 28 February 2015.

Selection of these four texts was dictated by what each one makes visible. Each interview falls into a different genre and captures a different moment. The *GQ* profile is long-form print journalism, which lets an interviewer probe a point and gives Bryant space for an extended analogy, so it shows the philosophy being argued. The Grantland conversation is a televised discussion with former players, and the only text where he talks about the making of the film, so it is where his language about authorship can be examined. The press conference is unscripted, public, and held under emotional pressure at the end of his career. The film is the only audiovisual account he produced and the only text in which he is also credited as a producer. Together they give two media, two moments of his career, and one text where the speaker is also a producer.

At first glance the opposition between self-representation and media representation looks obvious, until one examines the evidence. Bryant is credited as an executive producer of *Muse* (Chopra, 2015), and the finished film contains no interviews with anyone who might offer another point of view. The *GQ* interview also appeared eleven days before the premiere, inside the same publicity campaign.

Thus, the further discussion will consider *Muse* as a mediated self-portrait and will ask the research question in an improved way, if the interviews and the film represent the same self-representation of Bryant, what is the contribution of each medium to its formation? In this context, my hypothesis is based on the difference between content and form of the texts. The interviews provide a vocabulary and the analogies from which the Mamba Mentality is formed. *Muse* does not introduce any new idea, but gives them coherence, emotionality and inevitability in terms of chronology, archiving, cinematography and music.

# 2. The “Mamba Mentality” as a Constructed Concept

## 2.1 Origins and the Problem of Definition

Bryant adopted the persona of the Black Mamba in 2003, during the sexual assault case brought against him in Colorado. Bryant described his persona on camera in *Muse* as a creation that separated his playing self from the reputation that he had to lose (Chopra, 2015). He developed the idea of an alter ego first, and then associated his philosophical attitude towards basketball with it. As can be seen from the book, by the time he published *The Mamba Mentality: How I Play*, the phrase referred to a method and not to a nickname of the player. The book talks about a way of daily improvement based on comparison with the previous standards and the value of process over the results.

The book is interesting from the perspective of the definitional issue. It is organized around techniques such as footwork, preparation and the understanding of the opponent’s movements and strategy (Bryant & Bernstein, 2018). However, there is no sentence in the book where Mamba Mentality is defined.

There are two key points. First, the term had been applied retrospectively to something that had existed before the name; therefore, its definition has always been a retroactive interpretation. Second, it is ambiguous, having never been clearly defined by Bryant.

## 2.2 Why “Self” and “Media” Cannot Be Cleanly Separated

Hall (1997) states that representation does not convey meaning already existing but participates in its creation. This approach eliminates the possibility of debating whether Mamba Mentality represents the real Bryant or a media creation, because it denies the existence of a version of the Bryant that could be compared with the one created by representation. The question to ask is which resources each text employs.

This argument can be made stronger with the help of Dyer’s (1979/1998) theory. Dyer maintains that a star image is not contained in any specific text but constructed in the collection of many texts. In such a way, its consistency is achieved by its construction rather than being a personal characteristic. Mamba Mentality is one of such constructed images: it can be found in a profile, an interview, a press conference, a documentary and a book.

Bryant’s production activity makes this argument practical, and the evidence points two ways. At Showtime’s presentation to television critics in July 2014 he diminished his executive-producer credit, saying he had it “only in title alone” and that he did not know what a producer does; the Associated Press called the credit honorary, and Chopra denied that it carried any creative control (*Deadline*, 2014). Yet Chopra’s later recollection records that an editing suite was built in Newport Beach at Bryant’s insistence, near his house so that he could follow the process, and that Bryant paid close attention to the cutting, shot selection, scoring, colour correction and mixing (Chopra, 2021). The two accounts differ in scope rather than in content: Bryant could hold no formal power while still shaping the structure and the detail. Through the rest of my discussion I separate four things that self-representation blurs together: Bryant’s involvement, his self-presentation, the filmmakers’ construction, and the final text.

# 3. Constructing the Concept in the Interviews

## 3.1 Analogy as Self-Characterisation

What makes Bryant’s interview style unique is his self-description by analogies with non-basketball figures. There are three prominent examples from the *GQ* interview.

The first one is equine. Referring to his time with Phil Jackson, he says that he “was like a wild horse that had the potential to become Secretariat” (Klosterman, 2015). The simile depends on a contrast between present and potential: wild horse is what he was, Secretariat what he could be. The work is done by the verb phrase “had the potential to become”, which admits the wildness but re-classifies it as unrealised quality rather than flaw. The comparison also shifts responsibility, because a horse is not accountable for its own training; it needs a trainer, and Jackson occupies that role in the sentence. Bryant therefore concedes a difficulty while giving up nothing, since the difficulty is re-read as thoroughbred quality. For the research question this shows the concept being built out of concession rather than boast, which is what makes the Mamba Mentality read as earned.

The second is musical. Asked whether he shot too often, he says that “Some people thought Mozart had too many notes in his compositions,” and that “There were as many as necessary” (Klosterman, 2015). The device is substitution: a historical judgment already settled in Mozart’s favour replaces a contemporary one still under discussion, and the reader is left to draw the inference. The second sentence is also grammatically evasive, because “There were as many as necessary” has no agent; nobody decided the number, it simply was right. A statement that would sound arrogant in the first person becomes almost impossible to dispute, and the reader accepts a defence that was never explicitly made. This helps construct the concept by making excess look like necessity.

The third one is also used in the footnotes of the profile: Bryant denies the assumption that each brand must have its purpose: “That’s like saying every wizard with Slytherin House is a villain” (Klosterman, 2015). The device is reductio ad absurdum: the critic’s premise is restated in a form that is obviously wrong, and the reader rejects it without any effort on the side of the critic.

What is common for all three is a trick of returning criticism to a critic in the form of a category error. These devices are also similar in that they use a register that is not related to athletics: classical music, horse racing and children’s fiction. By using arguments about himself in non-athletic terms, Bryant makes the philosophy independent from its context of creation. The text is addressed to the reader who knows nothing about basketball. That is why the Mamba Mentality is constructed as something that can be transferred to other spheres.

## 3.2 Converting Behaviour into Identity

Another pattern of the interview material is Bryant’s statements about habits in terms that make them seem like something constitutive of his personality.

This is especially clear in the way he discusses friendship. In the *GQ* interview, asked whether his intensity leaves room for friends, he leans at first towards denying it, then corrects himself: “I have friends” (Klosterman, 2015). The interesting part follows. He distinguishes being a good friend from being a great one, sets the higher standard as everyday presence and remembered birthdays, and then admits that his obsession with work makes him forget such things. The mechanism is subordination, with the cost placed beneath the purpose it served. The structure accepts the accusation, establishes a standard Bryant does not reach, and explains the failure by dedication rather than character. As a result, the reader meets sacrifice as a conscious choice with a known cost rather than as something imposed on him.

The same transformation of an accusation into a concept is performed using pre-dawn training. The idea of 4 a.m. appears repeatedly in public statements of Bryant, including his discussion of his schedule in retirement (CNBC, 2018). The mechanism here is concrete specificity. Unlike such an abstraction as “dedication,” a clock time can be pictured, and repeated by someone else without modification. As a result, an assertion becomes a standard. Here the creation of the concept is driven less by arguments and more by choice of details.

Combining these mechanisms leads to a presentation of the Mamba Mentality not as a tool Bryant uses but as a characteristic of himself, and the distinction determines the nature of invitation from the text, while a tool invites evaluation, identity invites emulation.

## 3.3 Controlling the Account: The Grantland Interview

The conversation with Grantland is the only interview where Bryant discusses the documentary film as a made object. In response to the question why there are no mentions of Phil Jackson in it, he explains that he rejected a ready-to-go film made on the basis of forty external interviews and had the whole project done again, formulating his approach in four words: “control what you can control” (as reported in Yahoo Finance, 2015).

The phrase performs two functions at once. Grammatically it is a maxim: imperative mood, no subject, a construction that belongs to sayings and coaching slogans rather than to personal statements, and the repetition of “control” makes it sound like received advice rather than a decision he took. The context, however, is an explanation of how the film was edited. An act of control over the film is thereby delivered as stoicism, as acceptance of what cannot be changed, so that rebuilding the film around his own words looks like self-discipline rather than an editorial decision. This is the most revealing moment in the interview corpus, since it shows the Mamba Mentality being used to characterise the making of the film itself.

## 3.4 Vulnerability and the Retirement Press Conference

The press conference held on 29 November 2015 following the defeat to Indiana at home, where Bryant shot 4 of 20, is the text in which the self-characterisation comes under the greatest stress. On this day, Bryant retired via “Dear Basketball”, a letter that appeared on *The Players’ Tribune* (Bryant, 2015), before answering any questions.

When asked why he was retiring, he responded by turning the question back to himself:

> Do I want to play again or don’t I? It’s a very simple question, but it’s a hard question to look in the mirror and ask yourself. The reality is: No, I don’t. (as reported in *CNN*, 2015)

Four things happen here. First, he turns the interrogative from the reporter to himself, turning an interrogation into a self-reflection. Second, he contrasts “simple” and “hard” in one sentence, which shifts the source of the difficulty: the question is easy to comprehend but hard to ask oneself. Third, he changes the pronoun from “yourself” (generic), allowing the listeners to reflect upon themselves, and then returning to “I” for an answer, making the answer specific while the problem remains general. Fourth, after two complex sentences his reply consists of only three monosyllables, “No, I don’t”, thus embodying the decisiveness that is being claimed in the content with syntactic form, and the answer itself is preceded by “The reality is”, thus making it a fact rather than a choice.

The most crucial word used here is “want”. Bryant does not say he is unable to play anymore; he says he no longer wishes to. If he had decided to retire because his body was no longer able to keep up, Mamba Mentality would become a philosophy with a built-in expiry date. Since he is retiring because there is no longer the appetite to play, the mentality is kept untouched: it did not run out, the appetite did. This lexical choice is the one thing that makes it possible to detach the concept from the context in which it originated.

Nevertheless, this is a press conference; its answers come in response to a set of questions and in a form where a person is being interviewed. The text which seems to be the most straightforward text from the corpus is still a joint effort.

# 4. Constructing the Concept in *Kobe Bryant’s Muse*

## 4.1 Voice, and the Problem of Documentary Authority

One unique aspect of *Muse* is that Bryant is the only interviewee in this film. The entire documentary is based on his voice and narration with such personalities as Shaquille O’Neal, Phil Jackson and Michael Jordan appearing only in archives. It should be noted that initially, the film had another concept; Chopra spent several months making a traditional documentary with around forty interviews until Bryant declined (reported in Yahoo Finance, 2015).

As Nichols (2001) notes, the voice of a documentary’s subject differs from the voice of the documentary itself, which is expressed through the filmmaker’s choices. Removing the competing interviewees did not remove the film’s voice; it removed the most obvious proof that there is one. When a documentary cuts between witnesses, the audience can see an argument being built from different testimonies. Where only one witness speaks, the organisation of that argument becomes invisible and the interpretation is taken for the subject’s own. A constructed argument therefore comes across as testimony: nothing challenges Bryant, because the structure leaves no place from which he could be challenged.

The documentary does not avoid the problematic topics. Bryant talks about the 2003 Colorado case on camera, and on its aftermath he says that he “lost sight of what is the most important thing, and that’s family” (Chopra, 2015). The technique is confession delivered by the subject himself, in close-up. The result is an invitation to read the episode as a turning point rather than an open issue, since the grammar of “lost sight” presents the failing as a lapse of attention already corrected. Lowry (2015) calls the film “largely whitewashed” and judges that it passes over the episode too quickly. What matters here is not whether the coverage was adequate but how it works: the episode enters as a hardship survived and learned from, so that the Mamba Mentality is constructed as something forged by difficulty rather than merely asserted.

## 4.2 Three Sequences: Chronology, Image and Sound

If Bryant delivers the proposition, then the contribution of the film is its apparatus. There are three sequences which demonstrate it perfectly well.

(i) The Italian childhood. In terms of telling Bryant’s story, his Italian childhood relies not on the testimony given to the camera, but on the old footage (Chopra, 2015), the strongest stretch of the film according to Lowry (2015). It employs the device of archival montage. Because such images carry documentary authority, they lead viewers to take the interpretation given in the voiceover as confirmed rather than merely suggested. Thus, Bryant’s self-sufficiency, supposedly born during his isolation abroad, comes across as a fact proved by the film.

(ii) The shoulder injury. The film contains observational footage of Bryant being told about the rotator-cuff tear that ended his 2014-15 season (Chopra, 2015). The device is present-tense observation, with no retrospective narration. The effect is suspense in a story whose ending is already known, because the viewer watches someone receiving news rather than a legend recalling it. Testimony cannot do what this technique does, since the concept is shown being tested in real time rather than narrated afterwards.

(iii) The Achilles rehabilitation. The injury and the recovery are not presented in chronological order. The film moves associatively, placing the rehabilitation beside earlier events so that it reads as proof of a disposition already established. The rehabilitation footage is also shot in monochrome, which contrasts with the colour and texture of the archival game footage. Two devices work together here: associative editing and tonal separation. Barthes (1957/1972) defines myth as the process by which something contingent is made to seem essential and natural, and that is what the editing does, since an injury is an accident but, placed in this order, becomes an occasion for revealing an inherent quality. The monochrome does the same job, creating two orders of image, the public televised past and a private composed present, and giving the second interpretive authority over the first. The viewer is thereby offered a privileged interior, itself lit and framed, and it is suggested that the Mamba Mentality is not something Bryant adopted but something inherent.

In all three cases the music written by Kris Bowers accompanies the turning moments of Bryant’s life rather than the game action. Chion (1994) uses the concept of *added value* for the way sound creates meaning that viewers then attribute to the picture on the screen. The music does not tell the audience that an event is significant; it makes the audience feel the significance, which is then taken to belong to the event itself. While Bryant’s words argue that his discipline mattered, the music makes the viewer feel that importance directly, below the level at which a claim can be assessed.

Attributing these effects to Bryant is less straightforward than it looks. Bryant himself did not film, cut or score this film; Chopra and his collaborators did (Chopra, 2021). The form used in the film was neither imposed upon Bryant nor dictated by him, but negotiated in the room he created near his house.

# 5. Comparison and Synthesis

## 5.1 Continuities

Both the texts use identical elements to build the concept. Both construct Bryant’s life in terms of discipline, sacrifice and mastery; both favor concrete detail, such as training times and hours spent studying film, over abstraction and generalize, giving the impression of documented disposition; and both identify the Mamba Mentality as an identity, not as a practice.

## 5.2 Divergences

The distinction lies in the mode. The interviews are argumentative: Bryant argues, defends himself, concedes, repositions his arguments and therefore, provides explicit reasoning that can be examined. A reader could see, for example, that the Mozart analogy fails to address the question that Bryant sought to answer.

*Muse* is not argumentative. The claims in this case are conveyed through sequence, through images and music score. Claims expressed in this manner cannot be contested because they are not really stated at all. This explains why the film gives rise to a more coherent and non-examinable version of the concept. The distinction in interiority is not one that the “media flattens the person” account would have predicted: the press conference is the more exposed text, whereas the intimacy of the documentary is constructed.

## 5.3 The Extent of Each Contribution

The text types provide different contributions to the philosophy.

The interviews are the prerequisite of its existence. The analogies, process over outcome frame and identity grammar are born out of Bryant’s speech and without them, there would be nothing to organize into a philosophy. The weakness of the interviews lies in the fact that the interview language is diffuse, scattered and formed under the influence of individual agendas of various interviewers.

The contribution of *Muse* is the very form. It doesn’t bring any propositional meaning that is absent in the interviews, but it brings together coherence and emotional charge and makes it seem necessary. This is proven by the timing: the article in *GQ* came out on 17 February 2015, Bryant talked about the documentary on *Jimmy Kimmel Live!* on 23 February, and its premiere took place on 28 February. Three appearances in less than two weeks suggest elements of one release rather than separate texts.

However, there are two caveats to this thesis. First, the fact of having a production credit is not enough because the honorific nature of the credit has been publicly announced, while Bryant and Chopra state that he was very much involved. Second, while textual analysis can show how these texts work, it can’t explain their reception, and that is why this essay is confined to construction rather than to reception.

# 6. Conclusion

Under these conditions, the research question can be answered directly: both text types contribute considerably to constructing the “Mamba Mentality”, but they contribute different aspects, and the opposition between self-representation and media representation does not hold after analysis.

Interviews provide the content of the concept. Bryant creates it through language: analogies taken from outside basketball make his philosophy transcend the sport itself; criticisms conceded and returned as category errors make his identity seem deserved rather than claimed; in the conversation with Grantland, an act of editorial control is performed in the form of a maxim and thereby becomes part of the philosophy; and in explaining his decision to retire, Bryant uses “want” rather than “can”, thus grounding his decision in desire rather than in his physical condition.

Kobe Bryant’s *Muse* provides the form of self-definition, which makes it possible to be sustained for a long time. Having the credit of the executive producer, rejecting the initial version based on someone else’s testimony, and being the only interviewee, it cannot be interpreted as an outside representation. Still, it is not an unmediated self-representation. Archival montage turns a claim of origins into a document; footage of the diagnosis of his shoulder turns the concept into a testable idea; associative chronology and black-and-white filming turn contingency into essence; and Bowers’ score turns the viewing experience into the sense of importance of the event. None of these operations were made by Bryant, even though he might have been present at their conception.

Thus, the Mamba Mentality can be best explained as an image produced collaboratively via a mediation process. This image is mostly a product of Bryant’s work in terms of its content while, in its form, it is more likely to be attributed to the filmmakers, but belongs to none of them entirely. It is precisely because of such division of labour that the Mamba Mentality is durable. Bryant gave the concept something to say; the documentary gave it a shape in which saying was no longer necessary.
