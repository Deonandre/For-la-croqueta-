The Possession Paradox

Barça had the ball for roughly two thirds of every league match they played with Messi. Does knowing exactly how much of it they had tell you whether they won? An investigation built from 551,928 passes, a hand-built Elo rating system and more than a dozen statistical models.

      Chapter 1

      The short answer

      The question sounds simple: if you know how much of the ball Barcelona had, how well can you predict whether they won? For this team, in these seasons, the answer is that you can't. The reasons why are more interesting than the answer itself.

      This report follows the question from raw event data to a final verdict. It starts with the obvious test, builds the logistic regression model you would expect, finds nothing, and then keeps going. The rest of the report explains why possession looks useless, what is hiding underneath it, and what actually separated Barça's wins from their draws and defeats.

      1. 1
        On its own, possession has no link with winning.

        The correlation is −0.04. A logistic model gives an odds ratio of 0.89 per extra 10 percentage points of possession (95% CI 0.68–1.16). Tested season by season on unseen data, it predicts slightly worse than simply guessing Barça's usual win rate.

      2. 2
        The scoreline drives possession, not the other way round.

        Barça had 63.0% of the passes while leading, 68.2% while level and 70.2% while trailing. After scoring, their share fell from 67.8% to 64.4%. After conceding, it rose from 62.6% to 67.6%.

      3. 3
        Two forces cancel each other out.

        Against weaker teams Barça kept more of the ball and won more often, which makes possession look good. Score effects push the other way. Hold opponent strength fixed and more possession goes with a lower chance of winning (odds ratio 0.65, p = 0.006).

      4. 4
        Cleaner measures don't rescue it.

        Possession before the first goal, or while the score is level, looks mildly positive. That signal disappears when the last minute before each goal is removed. The passing move that produced the goal was doing all the work.

      5. 5
        Chance quality wins matches.

        xG difference (r = +0.47) and the shots-on-target difference (r = +0.43) are by far the strongest links to winning. In cross-validation, an xG model beats the base rate by 24%. Possession beats it by −0.5%.

      Verdict

      For Messi-era Barcelona, possession was a symptom of superiority and a defensive shield. Opponents who couldn't get the ball couldn't shoot. It was never a dial that turned wins up or down by itself. Knowing a match's possession figure tells you almost nothing about its result.

      Chapter 2

      The data

      Most possession studies start from a single number per match copied from a stats website. This one starts from every individual touch of the ball.

      Source

      StatsBomb publishes a free open-data release on GitHub. It includes every La Liga match Lionel Messi played for Barcelona between 2004/05 and 2020/21: 524 matches. Each match is a JSON file of about 3,000 to 4,000 events. Every pass, carry, pressure, tackle and shot is logged with a timestamp, the player, pitch coordinates and the outcome. Together the files come to 1.7 GB of raw data.

      Because every event is recorded, possession can be measured in several ways, split by scoreline, or measured only up to the first goal. A single final percentage can't do any of that, and those splits turn out to be the key to the whole question.

      Coverage and its bias

      Barcelona played 646 league matches across these 17 seasons. The open data covers 524 of them (81%), the ones Messi played in. That creates a selection effect. In this sample Barça won 73.7% of the time, compared with 70.6% across all 646 matches (456 wins). The findings describe Barcelona with Messi, which was a slightly better team than Barcelona overall. The early seasons are thin: 2004/05 has only seven matches, from when Messi was 17.

      Measuring possession

      "Possession" sounds like one number, but it can be calculated in different ways. Broadcasters and most data providers use pass share: a team's passes as a fraction of all passes in the match. This report uses that as its main measure.

      {{M:poss}}
      Main possession measure, calculated from every Pass event in the match (open play and set pieces).

      The alternative is in-play time. StatsBomb groups events into possession sequences, and summing how long each team held the ball in live play gives a time-based share. The two measures agree closely (r = 0.94), and every key test in this report gives the same answer with either one.

      Rating the opponents

      Beating Almería is not the same as beating Real Madrid. To account for this, I built an Elo rating system from scratch. It uses all 27,335 top-flight Spanish matches since 1929, from the open engsoccerdata archive. Each team gains or loses points after every match, depending on the result, the margin and how surprising the result was. The settings were tuned so the ratings predict results as well as possible: K = 15, a home advantage of 90 points, a 20% pull back towards average each summer, and promoted teams starting at 1450. The formulas are in the appendix.

      Before an average match in this sample, Barça were rated 1725 and their opponent 1491. Including home advantage, that is an edge of 234 points, which Elo turns into an expected score of 0.79.

      Data validation: the goals rebuilt from shot and own-goal events matched the official score in all 524 matches. Every one of the 524 matches was also linked to its row in the Elo archive by date, venue and score.
      Chapter 3

      Barça's possession world

      Before testing anything, it helps to see what "normal" looked like. For this team, dominating the ball wasn't an occasional tactic. It happened almost every week.

      Across the 524 matches, Barcelona's pass share averaged 65.9%, with a median of 66.8% and a standard deviation of 7.4 percentage points. The lowest was 44.0% and the highest 82.3%. They had less of the ball than the opponent in only 12 matches. That narrow range matters later: nearly every observation sits between 55% and 78%, so there is limited variation to explain anything with.

      Split by result, the averages are almost identical. Wins averaged 65.7%, draws 66.3% and defeats 66.4%. If anything, Barça had slightly more of the ball in the matches they lost.

      Seventeen seasons

      Possession peaked under Guardiola in 2010/11 at 70.7% and reached its lowest point in Valverde's first season, 2017/18, at 62.3%. Win rates moved around just as much, but not with possession. Valverde's lower-possession 2017/18 side won 75% of its matches in this sample and did not lose any of its 36. Koeman's 2020/21 side had more of the ball and won just 63%.

      Of the six matches with the most possession, Barça won three. Of the six with the least, they won four, all of them by three goals or more.

      Chapter 4

      The obvious test

      Group the matches by possession and count the wins. If possession helps, win rates should rise from left to right.

      The win rate goes 72.1% → 72.0% → 77.5% → 73.9% → 74.4% → 66.7% from the lowest band to the highest. There is no trend, and the highest band is the worst. The Wilson interval is used instead of the textbook ±1.96·SE because it behaves properly with proportions near 1 and small groups.

      Chapter 5

      Building the logistic model

      The standard tool for predicting a yes/no outcome from a number is logistic regression. Here it is built step by step and fitted to the data.

      Why logistic?

      A straight line can predict probabilities below 0 or above 1. The logistic function turns any value into a probability between 0 and 1, and its S-shape matches how probabilities usually behave: changes matter most in the middle and flatten near certainty. The model says the log-odds of winning are a straight-line function of possession.

      {{M:logistic}}
      The model: probability of a win given possession x (in percentage points).
      {{M:logit}}
      Equivalent form: the log-odds are linear in x. β₁ is the change in log-odds per percentage point.

      Fitting by maximum likelihood

      For each candidate pair (β₀, β₁), the model gives every match a win probability pi. The likelihood measures how well those probabilities agree with what actually happened. Maximum likelihood picks the pair that makes the observed results most probable. Working with the log of the likelihood turns the product into a sum.

      {{M:ll}}
      Log-likelihood over n = 524 matches, where yi = 1 for a win and 0 otherwise.

      There is no closed-form solution, so it's solved numerically. Gradient ascent repeatedly takes a small step uphill. Newton–Raphson also uses the curvature of the surface, and reaches the peak in a handful of jumps. The animation below runs both from the same deliberately poor starting point: a steep curve claiming possession is hugely important.

      {{M:newton}}

      Is the relationship curved?

      A straight line on the log-odds scale could miss a "sweet spot". Adding a squared term doesn't improve the fit significantly (likelihood-ratio p = 0.15). A LOWESS smoother lets the data draw its own curve. It rises gently to about 76% near 64% possession and then falls to about 62% at 80%. The bootstrap band is wide, though, and a flat line fits comfortably inside it.

      Chapter 6

      Why possession lies

      A final possession figure summarises 90 minutes in which the score kept changing. The score changes how teams play, so it also changes who has the ball.

      Think about what happens after Barça go 1–0 up. The opponent now has to attack, and Barça can afford to defend a lead. Possession drifts towards the team that is losing. When Barça fall behind, the opposite happens: the opponent sits deep, and Barça keep circulating the ball looking for a way through. So results partly cause possession, which is the reverse of the question being asked.

      Possession by score state

      Splitting every match into the periods when Barça were leading, level or trailing makes this directly visible. Across all matches, Barça had 63.0% of the passes while leading, 68.2% while level and 70.2% while trailing. By in-play time the figures are 62.0%, 69.2% and 70.7%.

      The within-match comparison is the most convincing. There were 423 matches with both a leading and a level period. In those, Barça's share was 5.7 points lower while leading (paired t = −14.3, p ≈ 10−38), and lower in 76% of the matches. In the 129 matches with both a trailing and a level period, it was 4.7 points higher while trailing (p ≈ 10−8).

      What happens the moment a goal goes in

      An event study lines up all 1,387 Barça goals and all 438 goals they conceded at minute zero. It then measures pass share minute by minute for the 15 minutes before and after. Each window is cut off at any neighbouring goal, so every minute is measured in a single score state.

      Across the 90 minutes

      The same pattern shows over the whole match. In games Barça went on to win, they started with about 70% of the passes and finished on 56%, as they protected leads and opponents pushed forward. In draws and defeats their share stayed between 61% and 72% throughout, because they were still chasing.

      Chapter 7

      The two forces

      If score effects push the possession–win link downwards, something must be pushing it back up, because the raw correlation is zero rather than negative. That something is the quality of the opponent.

      Against strong teams Barça had less of the ball: possession falls with opponent Elo (r = −0.32, p ≈ 10−14). They also won less often. Against weak teams they had more of the ball and won more. Opponent strength therefore creates a positive link between possession and winning. At the same time, score effects create a negative one. The two roughly cancel.

      {{M:forces}}
      The raw correlation is close to zero because two opposite effects are added together.

      Holding opponent strength fixed

      Split the opponents into thirds by Elo, then split each third into Barça's low, middle and high possession matches. Within the same level of opponent, the positive strength effect is removed and the negative score effect is left. Against the weakest third, Barça won 91.5% of their lowest-possession matches and just 63.8% of their highest-possession matches. When a weak side kept Barça out, Barça ended up with lots of the ball and no goal to show for it.

      Adding controls one at a time

      The clearest way to see the two forces is to add the confounders to the logistic model one by one and follow the possession coefficient. Adding venue changes little. Adding the Elo edge makes possession significantly negative, with an odds ratio of 0.65 per 10 points (p = 0.006). This is the score effect with the strength effect removed. Adding how many minutes Barça spent trailing, and then leading, brings the coefficient back to 1.08 (p = 0.72). Once the model knows how strong the opponent was and how the scoreline developed, possession adds nothing.

      Chapter 8

      Cleaning the measurement

      If the scoreline contaminates possession, measure possession before the scoreline can affect it. This chapter tries that. It seems to work at first, and then it doesn't.

      Two cleaner measures

      Possession before the first goal uses only the passes played while the match was 0–0. For a stable estimate, matches needed at least 100 passes before the first goal (454 of 524). Possession while level uses every period when the scores were equal.

      Both measures now point the expected way. Pre-goal possession gives an odds ratio of 1.27 per 10 points (p = 0.053). Possession while level gives 1.35 (p = 0.011). Scoring first is the likely route: Barça opened the scoring in 75% of matches with a goal, and pre-goal possession strongly predicts who scores first (odds ratio 1.64 per 10 points, p = 0.0002).

      The catch: the goal's own build-up

      The event study in Fig. 16 shows a problem. In the minute before Barça score, they have 77.9% of the passes, because the move that creates a goal is a passing move. Any "possession before the first goal" measure therefore includes the build-up to that goal. A team whose first goal came from a long passing move automatically gets a higher pre-goal possession figure and scores first. Part of the link is built into the measurement.

      The fix is simple. Remove the last g minutes before the first goal (or before every goal, for the "while level" measure) and see what's left.

      {{M:pregap}}
      Possession before the first goal at time t₁, excluding a gap of g minutes of build-up.

      The build-up drove most of the apparent effect. Once it's removed, possession before the first goal and possession while level carry no reliable information about the result. A small trace remains in who scores first (odds ratio 1.31 at a 1-minute gap, p = 0.046), but that also disappears after adjusting for Elo (1.12, p = 0.43). In the end, the better team kept the ball and scored first. Possession was a symptom of that, not the cause.

      Threshold check: requiring 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 300 or 400 pre-goal passes gives unadjusted odds ratios between 1.23 and 1.66. With no threshold the effect is strongest (1.37, p = 0.0005), because very early goals make pre-goal possession almost entirely build-up.

      Chapter 9

      What actually wins

      If possession isn't the answer, what is? I ranked 20 match statistics by their correlation with winning.

      Possession as defence

      Possession did change one thing: how much the opponent could do. More possession went with fewer opponent shots (r = −0.46) and less xG conceded (r = −0.25). It had almost no link with Barça's own xG (r = +0.06, p = 0.19). Opponents can't shoot without the ball, so for Barça possession worked as a shield more than a weapon.

      How the statistics relate to each other

      Possession alongside shots on target

      What about a model that combines possession with shots on target? With Barça's shots on target alone, the possession coefficient is −0.028 per point (p = 0.059). Adding the opponent's shots on target makes it −0.061 (odds ratio 0.54 per 10 points, p = 0.0003). For the same number of shots at both ends, more possession means lower chances of winning. If Barça needed more of the ball to create the same chances, they were usually chasing the game.

      Chapter 10

      The prediction contest

      Fitting a model and checking it on the same data flatters every model. The fair test is prediction on matches the model has never seen.

      I used leave-one-season-out cross-validation. Each model is trained on 16 seasons and asked to predict every match of the 17th, and this repeats 17 times. Predictions are scored three ways. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the probabilities. Log loss penalises confident mistakes heavily. AUC is the chance that a randomly chosen win gets a higher predicted probability than a randomly chosen non-win. The Brier skill score compares each model with guessing the training-set win rate every time.

      {{M:brier}}
      Brier score and Brier skill score against a reference forecast.
      {{M:logloss}}
      Log loss (cross-entropy). Lower is better.

      Both possession-only models lose to the base rate (skill −0.5%, AUC 0.48 and 0.45). Pre-goal possession scrapes +0.4%. The best pre-match model, Elo edge plus venue, reaches +6.0%. Models using in-match chance statistics do far better: shots on target +20.0%, xG for and against +23.7%, and everything combined +31.6% (AUC 0.84). The in-match models explain results after the fact, since you only know a match's xG once it's over. Even so, possession comes last among them too.

      {{M:auc}}
      AUC as a probability: how often the model ranks a real win above a real non-win.
      Chapter 11

      Where the ball goes

      Possession is also about where on the pitch the passes happen. Mapping them shows what high possession actually looked like for Barça.

      In high-possession matches (the top quartile, 71.3–82.3%), Barça played 817 passes on average against 579 in low-possession matches (44.0–60.6%). The extra passes weren't spread evenly. Only 16.0% were in Barça's own third, compared with 25.8% in low-possession games, and 28.9% were in the final third, compared with 21.5%. Opponents in those matches were pushed back: 34.9% of their passes came from their own third, against 26.4%.

      Comparing wins with defeats, the pass maps look very similar. In defeats a slightly larger share of Barça's passes were in the final third (27.5% against 24.8%), which fits a team spending those matches chasing. The shot maps show a clearer difference. Barça took almost as many shots in defeats (14.6 per match) as in wins (15.9), but those shots were worth only 1.32 xG against 2.03. The gap comes mostly from the central zone in and around the box.

      Chapter 12

      Eras and coaches

      Eight head coaches, one tradition of keeping the ball. Each one's possession and results show the same mismatch.

      Guardiola's teams had 67.6% of the ball, won 77.9% of their matches and lost only 6.6%. Setién's short spell in 2020 had the highest possession of any coach (69.2%) and won 68.4%. Valverde's teams had the lowest possession since Rijkaard (63.1%), yet lost the smallest share of matches of anyone: 4.8%. Koeman's 2020/21 team kept 65.3% of the ball but lost 20% of its matches in this sample, the highest of any coach.

      How the style changed

      The way Barça used the ball changed more than the amount of it. The average passing sequence grew from 5.2 passes in 2004/05 to 8.4 in 2020/21, and sequences of 10 or more passes almost doubled, from 15 to 28 a match. At the same time pressing got less intense: PPDA rose from 9.4 to 15.8. Later Barça teams kept the ball longer and chased it less. xG conceded roughly doubled over the same period, from 0.52 to 1.01 per match.

      Chapter 13

      Stress tests

      A conclusion that "nothing is there" deserves the same scrutiny as a positive one. Here the key estimates go through resampling, shuffling and ten alternative analysis choices.

      Bootstrap

      The bootstrap resamples the 524 matches with replacement, refits the model and repeats this 2,000 times. The spread of the refitted odds ratios shows how much the estimate could vary by chance, without relying on formulas that assume normality.

      Permutation test

      A permutation test asks how often a correlation this large would appear if possession and results were completely unrelated. Shuffle the win labels at random, recompute the correlation, and repeat 5,000 times. The share of shuffled correlations at least as large as the real one is the p-value. It needs no distributional assumptions at all.

      {{M:perm}}
      Two-sided permutation p-value from B = 5,000 shuffles.

      Ten alternative analyses

      The whole-match result doesn't depend on any single choice of subset, measure or outcome. No version gives a significantly positive effect. The only significant one is negative, once team strength is controlled.

      Chapter 14

      Simulating seasons

      A win/no-win model throws away draws and scorelines. A goals model keeps both. It can then simulate whole seasons under different possession levels.

      A Poisson goals model

      Goals in football are rare, roughly independent events, which is what a Poisson distribution describes. I fitted one Poisson regression for goals scored and one for goals conceded. Each used possession, Elo edge and venue. The data suit the model well: the dispersion ratios are 0.99 and 0.96, where 1.0 is a perfect Poisson fit.

      Monte Carlo: 10,000 seasons per scenario

      Fix Barça's possession at 55%, 65% or 75%. Draw 38 opponents at random from the real schedule (with their real Elo gaps and venues), simulate each scoreline from the Poisson model, and add up the points. Repeat 10,000 times. The three scenarios produce 92.1, 90.4 and 88.9 points on average. That is a gap of about 3 points, and each distribution has a spread of about ±6.6 points. For reference, Barça's actual rate across these matches works out to 90.2 points per 38 games.

      Chapter 15

      Conclusions

      Answering the research question

      To what extent can possession percentage be used to model the probability of FC Barcelona winning a match? In the 524 La Liga matches Messi played between 2004 and 2021, almost not at all. A logistic model built on possession is statistically indistinguishable from a flat line. In fair out-of-sample testing it does worse than guessing Barça's usual win rate. That holds whether possession is measured by passes or by time, whether draws count as failures or successes, and in every era and subset tested.

      Why the model fails

      1. Reverse causation. The scoreline shapes possession. Leading teams give up the ball and trailing teams chase it, so a defeat produces a high possession figure.
      2. Confounding. Opponent quality lowers both Barça's possession and their chance of winning. That creates a positive link that happens to cancel the negative one.
      3. Measurement leakage. Possession measured "before the goal" includes the goal's own build-up. Removing a single minute removes the apparent effect.
      4. Restricted range. Barça had more of the ball in 97.7% of matches. Almost all the variation is between 55% and 78%, so there is little room for possession to separate outcomes.

      What possession was for

      The data points to possession as defence for this team. Every extra point of possession meant fewer opponent shots, and Barça's own chance creation stayed about the same. It was also a sign of superiority: better Barça teams against weaker opponents kept more of the ball. What decided matches was the quality of chances at each end. That's why xG difference and shots-on-target difference top every ranking.

      Limitations

      • Selection. Only matches Messi played are covered (81% of league games). A Barça without Messi might show different patterns.
      • One team. Barcelona is an outlier that almost always had the ball. Across a whole league, where possession varies from 30% to 70%, the relationship can look different.
      • Correlation, not causation. Every model here is observational. The simulations in Chapter 14 are thought experiments, not predictions of what a tactical change would do.
      • Measurement choices. Possession is pass share or sequence time, xG is StatsBomb's model, and the Elo ratings are built from results only. Each is reasonable but not unique.
      • Binary outcome. The main model treats draws and defeats as the same. The Poisson model and the "avoid defeat" check address this, with the same conclusion.

      Where to go next

      Natural extensions would be to compute possession value (expected threat) instead of possession volume, model every La Liga team with a multilevel model, and use StatsBomb 360 freeze frames to measure where the opponent's players were when Barça had the ball. The question those would answer is not "how much ball?" but "how dangerous was the ball?".

      One sentence

      For Messi's Barcelona, possession was the outcome of dominance and the scoreline, not a predictor of the result: the ball followed the game.
